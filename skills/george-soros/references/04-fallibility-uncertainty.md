# Human Fallibility and Radical Uncertainty

> When to read this file: when thinking about the epistemological foundations of investment decisions, when assessing how confident to be in a thesis, when the situation involves genuine uncertainty (not calculable risk), when examining how imperfect human knowledge interacts with financial markets, or when considering how widely shared beliefs can change the very reality they describe.

George Soros's intellectual framework rests on two foundational pillars. Reflexivity (covered in 01-reflexivity.md) is the market condition. **Fallibility** is the human condition. Together they explain why financial markets behave as they do: humans cannot achieve perfect knowledge, and their imperfect knowledge-driven actions change the reality they are trying to understand.

Soros developed his thinking under the influence of philosopher Karl Popper, whose open society theory and critical rationalism provided the philosophical scaffolding for Soros's investment worldview.

> "My own framework rests on two pillars. One is that our understanding of the world in which we live is inherently imperfect. The other is that these imperfect views affect the situation they relate to — they do not merely reflect it. I call the first fallibility and the second reflexivity." — George Soros, *The New Paradigm for Financial Markets* (2008)

## Karl Popper and the Foundation of Soros's Thinking

Soros studied under Karl Popper at the London School of Economics in the 1950s. Popper's two great contributions — falsificationism and the Open Society — became the intellectual DNA of Soros's investment and philanthropic work.

**Popper's falsificationism:**
A theory is scientific not because it can be verified (proven true) but because it can be falsified (proven false). All human knowledge is provisional — the best we can say about a theory is that it has not yet been disproven. This applies equally to scientific theories and investment theses.

> "Popper taught me that our understanding of the world will always be imperfect. We can never be certain that our theories are correct; we can only be certain when they are wrong. This has profound implications for how one should act under uncertainty — and investing is always an exercise in acting under uncertainty." — Soros, various speeches

**What this means for investing:**
1. Every investment thesis is a hypothesis, not a fact.
2. The goal is not to find the "correct" thesis but to find one that has not yet been disproven by evidence.
3. New evidence that contradicts the thesis must be taken seriously, not explained away.
4. The thesis must be structured so it can be tested — a thesis that can explain any outcome is not a useful thesis.

## The Human Uncertainty Principle

Soros draws an analogy between physics and social science:

In physics, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that the act of measuring a subatomic particle changes the particle's behavior. The observer cannot be separated from the observed.

In social science (including financial markets), the equivalent principle operates at a much larger scale: **the act of theorizing about social systems changes those systems**. When economists publish a model of market behavior, that model — if widely adopted — changes market behavior in ways the original model did not anticipate.

> "There is a reflexive relationship between the participant and the situation, just as there is between the investigator and the subject of investigation in quantum physics. But in social science, the interference is not just an unfortunate by-product of measurement — it is the essence of the phenomenon. The human uncertainty principle is the foundation of my investment theory." — Soros, *The Alchemy of Finance* (1987)

**Practical consequence:** Widely-used models eventually break themselves:
- **The "Fed Put" model**: The belief that the Fed will always rescue markets is itself a moral hazard that causes risk-taking that eventually forces the Fed to choose between inflation and systemic collapse
- **Value at Risk (VaR)**: When all banks use the same VaR model, they all reduce positions simultaneously during stress, creating the very correlated collapse the model was designed to prevent
- **Efficient market assumptions**: When investors use index funds universally, capital allocation becomes disconnected from company fundamentals, creating distortions that reintroduce exactly the alpha opportunities the theory says should not exist

## Radical Uncertainty vs. Calculable Risk

Soros distinguishes between two fundamentally different types of ignorance:

### Calculable Risk (Knight's "Risk")
Situations where the probability distribution of outcomes is known or can be estimated from historical data. Example: a coin flip, actuarial tables, option pricing for liquid instruments.

**Soros's view:** Financial models built on calculable risk misrepresent the nature of most investment decisions. Standard deviation, VaR, and normal distribution assumptions treat financial markets as if they were like actuarial tables — stable probability distributions from which you can estimate tail risk.

### Radical Uncertainty (Knight's "Uncertainty")
Situations where the probability distribution of outcomes is not known and cannot be estimated reliably. Example: political regime changes, technological disruptions, reflexive boom-bust cycles.

> "Radical uncertainty is not just a practical problem of insufficient data. It is a fundamental feature of complex, reflexive systems. In a reflexive system, the future is not already determined — it is being created by the beliefs and actions of participants. You cannot calculate the probability distribution of something that does not yet exist." — Soros, speeches

**The critical implication for position sizing:**

Under calculable risk: position size according to expected value and probability-weighted loss scenarios.

Under radical uncertainty (Soros's actual world): position size according to what you can afford to lose if completely wrong, NOT according to your probability estimate. If you don't know the probability distribution, no mathematical calculation gives you the "right" position size — only your loss tolerance does.

## Fallibility in Practice: What Soros Does Differently

Because Soros accepts fallibility as fundamental, his analytical process is explicitly designed to identify and correct for bias:

### 1. Starting with a Hypothesis, Not a Conclusion

> "I start with the assumption that my hypothesis is wrong, and I look for evidence to disprove it. If I cannot disprove it, I become more confident — but never certain." — Soros, various interviews

This is the opposite of most investment analysis, which starts with a bullish or bearish conclusion and then marshals evidence to support it. Soros's process is falsificationist: he tries to kill the thesis first. If it survives, he acts on it.

### 2. Treating the Current Thesis as Provisional

> "I am not the right person to ask about my positions. I don't know what position I'll have tomorrow. I hold my views very loosely. If the evidence changes, I change my view. If the evidence doesn't change but my interpretation of the evidence changes, I change my view." — Soros, *Soros on Soros* (1995)

This radical flexibility — what traders might call "having no ego" — is a direct expression of fallibilism. A fallibilist does not become attached to their hypothesis because they know it is provisional.

### 3. The Aching Back Indicator

Soros has famously described using physical sensations as signals of when his investment thesis is creating excessive stress:

> "When I am under stress, I get backaches. The backache is a signal — it means my positions are too large relative to my conviction, or my conviction is too large relative to the actual evidence. I have come to trust physical signals as indicators that something in my analysis is wrong that I haven't yet consciously identified." — Soros, various interviews

This is not mysticism — it is the recognition that physical stress signals have information about one's actual (vs. stated) confidence level.

### 4. Updating on New Evidence (Genuine Bayesian Updating)

> "What I know is that I don't know. What I can do is update my ignorance systematically as new evidence arrives." — Soros, interviews

Unlike most investors who update slowly (due to commitment bias), Soros updates quickly and dramatically. If a position is not working as the thesis predicts, he cuts it — not because the loss is painful, but because the market's failure to confirm the thesis is itself evidence against the thesis.

## The Impossibility of Perfect Knowledge: Implications for Investment

**The paradox of investment certainty:**
The more certain you are about an investment, the more likely it is that:
1. Your certainty is the result of selecting confirming evidence and ignoring disconfirming evidence
2. Your certainty is widely shared (consensus), which means the thesis is already priced in
3. Your certainty has caused you to over-size the position beyond what genuine uncertainty would permit

**Soros's resolution:** Trade size is the primary tool for expressing your actual (not stated) degree of certainty. Small position = I have an interesting hypothesis. Medium position = the evidence so far is supportive. Large position = the market has confirmed the thesis over multiple tests.

> "The size of a position should reflect the quality of the thesis and the weight of evidence, not the strength of feeling. I have had very strong feelings about investments that turned out to be wrong, and I have had uncertain, tentative views about investments that turned out to be extraordinarily profitable." — Soros, interviews

## Popper's Open Society and Market Ideology

Soros extends Popper's political philosophy to financial markets. Popper argued that "open societies" — those that allow criticism and adaptation — are superior to "closed societies" that enforce ideological conformity.

Soros applies this to economic ideology: market fundamentalism (the belief that unregulated markets always produce optimal outcomes) is as dangerous as any other totalitarian ideology because it is immune to criticism by its own internal logic.

> "Market fundamentalism is today a greater threat to open society than any totalitarian ideology. The doctrine that markets should be allowed to run free is just as open to the charge of dogmatism as the doctrines of socialism or communism. All three suffer from the same epistemological error: they claim certainty in a world that is fundamentally uncertain." — Soros, *The Crisis of Global Capitalism* (1998)

**The investment implication:** Periods of maximum ideological consensus about economic policy (whether free-market or interventionist) are precisely the periods when the policy is most likely to be taken to an extreme that eventually reverses. Ideological consensus → far-from-equilibrium policy → reflexive reversal.

## Practical Checklist: Applying Fallibility to Investment Decisions

Before committing significant capital, answer these questions:

**Falsifiability:**
- [ ] Is my thesis falsifiable? What specific evidence would prove it wrong?
- [ ] Have I actively sought that disconfirming evidence, or only the confirming kind?
- [ ] Has the evidence I dismissed as "noise" actually been disconfirming my thesis consistently?

**Uncertainty calibration:**
- [ ] Am I dealing with calculable risk (estimable distribution) or radical uncertainty?
- [ ] Is my position size consistent with genuine uncertainty, or does it reflect over-confidence?
- [ ] If I were completely wrong, how much would I lose? Is that loss tolerable?

**Consensus and pricing:**
- [ ] Is my thesis consensus or contrarian?
- [ ] If it is consensus, is it already priced in?
- [ ] If it is contrarian, what do the people who disagree know that I might be missing?

**Model validity:**
- [ ] Am I applying a model developed in near-equilibrium conditions to a far-from-equilibrium situation?
- [ ] Is the model I am using so widely used that its adoption has changed the behavior it describes?

**Update readiness:**
- [ ] Have I defined in advance what evidence would cause me to change my thesis?
- [ ] Am I prepared to act on that evidence quickly, or will I rationalize it away?
