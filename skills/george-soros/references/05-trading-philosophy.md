# Soros's Trading Philosophy

> When to read this file: when deciding how to size a position, when considering whether to add to or reduce a position, when a trade is not working as expected and you need a framework for deciding whether to hold or cut, when thinking about risk management in macro trading, or when studying how Soros translates his theoretical conviction into actual portfolio management.

George Soros's trading philosophy is as distinctive as his theory of reflexivity, and the two are deeply connected. Because Soros believes in human fallibility and radical uncertainty, his trading process is designed for a world where being wrong is the default assumption and being right is the happy exception that you capitalize on aggressively when it occurs.

The central tension in Soros's approach is between intellectual conviction (which can be strong) and position size (which must reflect genuine uncertainty). He resolves this tension through a specific process: start small, test the thesis, add aggressively when the market confirms, and exit quickly when it doesn't.

> "The way to build long-term returns is not to be right more often than you are wrong. It is to be right in a big way when you are right, and to be wrong in a small way when you are wrong. That asymmetry is the whole game." — George Soros, *Soros on Soros* (1995)

---

## The Core Process: Test → Confirm → Scale

### Step 1: Test the Thesis with a Small Position

Before committing significant capital, Soros establishes a "test position" — small enough that being wrong is merely a learning experience, not a financial crisis.

> "When I have a hypothesis, I test it with a small position. If the position loses money immediately, I learn something: either the timing is wrong, or the thesis is wrong. Either way, the information is more valuable than the paper loss." — Soros, interviews

**The purpose of the test position:**
- It is not to generate returns. It is to generate information.
- A test position that makes money quickly confirms the thesis — add aggressively.
- A test position that loses money confirms either bad timing or a bad thesis — investigate before adding.
- A test position that does nothing may indicate the thesis is too early — wait.

**How small is "small"?**
Soros's definition of a test position is relative to his maximum intended position. If the full position would be 20% of the fund, the test position might be 3-5%. At that size, even a 30% adverse move on the test position is less than 2% of fund NAV — a learning experience, not a catastrophe.

---

### Step 2: Pyramid When Right

When the market confirms the thesis — prices move in the predicted direction, fundamentals develop as expected, the reflexive feedback loop begins to engage — Soros adds to the position aggressively.

> "I'm only rich because I know when I'm wrong. I've basically survived by recognizing my mistakes. But knowing when you're right is equally important — when you're right, you need to be right in a big way. That means adding when the position is working, not just holding." — Soros, various interviews

**The pyramid structure:**
- Test position (smallest): establish initial exposure, gather information
- Confirmation add (medium): market confirms the thesis; add substantially
- Conviction add (largest increment): thesis fully confirmed over multiple tests; maximum intended exposure

**Why pyramid rather than buy all at once?**
- Avoids full exposure to the uncertainty of being early (Phase 1-2 of the boom-bust cycle)
- Ensures that the largest positions are built at prices that the market has already confirmed
- Creates a natural position-averaging that is upward (if right) rather than downward (if wrong)
- The cost of this approach: missing the first leg of the move; the benefit: much lower risk of a catastrophic early entry error

---

### Step 3: Never Average Down — Cut and Reassess

The single most important trading rule in Soros's philosophy:

> "If a position isn't working, I don't add to it. I cut it. My job is not to be right — it is to make money. Those are not the same thing. If the market is telling me I'm wrong, I listen." — Soros, *The Alchemy of Finance* (1987)

**Averaging down** (adding to a losing position) is the most common error in discretionary investing. The psychological appeal is obvious: if you liked the investment at $100, you should love it at $80. But Soros's reflexivity framework reveals why this reasoning is wrong:

- In a reflexive downturn (Phase 7-8 of the boom-bust cycle), falling prices are not revealing bargains — they are changing the fundamentals. The company's credit, customer confidence, supplier relationships, and management's ability to execute all deteriorate as the price falls.
- The market's failure to confirm your thesis IS information. Specifically, it is evidence that either your thesis is wrong or your timing is wrong.
- Both errors require the same response: reduce exposure, not increase it.

**The discipline of cutting:**
Soros's ability to cut quickly — and accept being wrong — is what has made him one of the most durable macro investors in history. His worst losses (Russia 1998, tech bubble) all involved a period of holding or adding to losing positions before finally cutting. His best trades (pound 1992, yen carry trade) involved pyramiding into winners.

## Survival as Strategy: The Priority Stack

Soros's priority stack in descending order:

1. **Survival**: Never take a position large enough that a single bad outcome can permanently impair the fund.
2. **Optionality**: Maintain enough dry powder (cash and position capacity) to act on the best opportunities.
3. **Profitability**: Generate returns when the thesis is confirmed.

Note that profitability is THIRD. This seems counterintuitive for a fund manager, but it is the correct hierarchy. Without survival and optionality, profitability cannot be sustained over decades.

> "My first concern is not to make money but to not lose money. The returns come from the opportunities I don't miss because I was too damaged by a prior loss. Survival is not a conservative strategy — it is the highest-return strategy over long time periods." — Soros, *Soros on Soros* (1995)

**Practical implications:**

- **Maximum position size:** Soros has described his maximum single position as 30-35% of the fund at times of very high conviction (pound 1992). But this is the exception, not the rule — and it required multiple layers of confirmation before he reached that size.

- **Portfolio correlation:** When all positions express the same macro thesis (e.g., all are bearish on European currencies in 1992), the effective position in the thesis is the sum of the correlated positions. Soros actively manages this: when his book is highly correlated, he treats the entire correlated portfolio as a single bet.

- **Drawdown limits:** Unlike many hedge funds with hard drawdown triggers, Soros's approach is more discretionary — but the principle is the same: at some level of loss, the thesis must be reassessed from zero.

## The Survival Instinct: Reading the Market's Signals

Soros describes a particularly important psychological state he achieves when a trade is working against him:

> "When I am in the middle of a losing trade, I have developed what I call the survival instinct. I focus entirely on the question: if I had no position right now, would I put this one on? If the answer is no, I get out immediately. The question forces me to separate the sunk cost of the current loss from the forward-looking decision about whether this is a good trade." — Soros, interviews

**The "would I put it on today?" test:**
This simple question cuts through commitment bias, loss aversion, and the natural human reluctance to crystallize a paper loss. It forces a forward-looking assessment:
- Is the thesis still valid?
- Is the entry point now better or worse than when I established the position?
- Would an unbiased observer, seeing the current situation fresh, establish this trade?

If the answer is no — exit. The current loss is irrelevant to the decision.

## Macro Trading: Instruments and Leverage

Soros's approach to macro trading involves using multiple instruments to express a single thesis, and using leverage judiciously.

**Instrument selection for macro trades:**
- **Currencies**: Direct expression of macro thesis about monetary regimes; very liquid; can be shorted easily via FX forwards or options
- **Interest rate futures**: Express views on central bank policy trajectory; enormous liquidity
- **Equity indices**: Proxy for overall economic health; less precise than currencies but useful for correlated exposure
- **Commodities**: Express views on global growth, dollar trajectory, supply dynamics
- **Individual stocks**: Used when the macro thesis has a very specific company-level expression; higher idiosyncratic risk

**On leverage:**

> "I use leverage. I think leverage is an essential tool for a macro fund — you cannot generate competitive returns in liquid, efficient macro markets without leverage. But leverage must be used in inverse proportion to uncertainty. When my conviction is highest, I am most willing to add leverage. When I am uncertain, I run flat or even short." — Soros, interviews

Soros's leverage discipline:
- Leverage is a multiplier: it amplifies both gains and losses
- Maximum leverage in his historical operations: 2-3x gross exposure as a fraction of NAV in normal operations; temporarily higher during very high conviction macro positions
- Leverage is not a substitute for conviction — it is an amplifier of conviction. Using leverage to compensate for a weak thesis is the most dangerous error

**Option use for convex expression:**
When the asymmetry of a trade is very high (small probability of large gain, or large probability of small gain with fat-tail loss protection needed), Soros uses options:
- Buying puts on currencies or indices allows participation in a crash thesis with defined risk
- This is particularly useful when the timing of a Phase 7-8 transition is uncertain

## Psychological Edge: Living with Uncertainty

Soros's psychological framework is designed for a world of radical uncertainty:

> "My approach to managing risk is essentially psychological. I try to maintain an awareness of the potential for being wrong, and I try to structure my portfolio so that being wrong does not destroy me. Most people cannot do this because they cannot tolerate the anxiety of uncertainty. I have learned to use that anxiety productively." — Soros, various interviews

**The "backache indicator" revisited:**
Physical discomfort during a trade is informational. It usually signals one of:
1. The position is too large relative to genuine conviction
2. The thesis has been quietly contradicted by evidence that the conscious mind hasn't fully processed yet
3. The risk profile of the position has changed (correlation changed; liquidity changed)

When Soros notices physical stress, he examines the portfolio first for position sizing problems, second for thesis validity.

**Intellectual honesty as competitive advantage:**
The ability to say "I was wrong" quickly and without ego damage is perhaps Soros's deepest competitive edge. Most fund managers cannot admit a mistake without a lengthy period of rationalization, during which the losing position continues to deteriorate. Soros exits immediately — and has described this as the single most important skill in his trading career.

> "I'm not smarter than the market. But I am faster to recognize when I am wrong. That speed is everything." — Soros, interviews

| Cut quickly when the thesis is disproven | Sunk costs are irrelevant; survival requires speed | Various interviews |
