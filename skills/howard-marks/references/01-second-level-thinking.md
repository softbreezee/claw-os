# Second-Level Thinking

> When to read this file: when you need to understand what it takes to outperform the market, when analyzing whether the consensus view on an investment is correct, when thinking about what is already priced in, or when discussing the concept of variant perception.

This is Marks's foundational concept — the single idea from which his entire investment philosophy flows.

> "First-level thinking is simplistic and superficial, and just about everyone can do it. All the first-level thinker needs is an opinion about the future. Second-level thinking is deep, complex and convoluted." — The Most Important Thing

---

## First-Level vs. Second-Level Thinking

### The Core Distinction

**First-level thinking** says: "This is a good company. Let's buy the stock."
**Second-level thinking** says: "This is a good company. But everyone thinks it's a great company, and it's not. So it's overrated and overpriced; let's sell."

**First-level thinking** says: "The outlook calls for low growth and rising inflation. Let's dump our stocks."
**Second-level thinking** says: "The outlook stinks, but everyone else is selling in panic. Buy!"

**First-level thinking** says: "I think the company's earnings will drop; sell."
**Second-level thinking** says: "I think the company's earnings will drop less than people expect, and the pleasant surprise will lift the stock; buy."

> "The problem is that extraordinary performance comes only from correct nonconsensus forecasts, but nonconsensus forecasts are hard to make, hard to make correctly, and hard to act on." — The Most Important Thing

### Why First-Level Thinking Cannot Produce Superior Results

The market is the consensus of all investors. If your thinking is the same as everyone else's, you will get average results — before fees, which means below-average results after fees.

**The mathematical reality:**
- The market return is the average return of all investors combined
- To beat the average, you must think differently AND be right
- Thinking differently but being wrong is worse than thinking like everyone else
- Thinking like everyone else guarantees mediocrity

> "You can't do the same things others do and expect to outperform." — Oaktree Memo, "Dare to Be Great"

### The Requirements for Superior Returns

Marks identifies three requirements that must ALL be met:

1. **You must hold a view that differs from the consensus** — If your view is the same as everyone else's, it's already in the price
2. **Your non-consensus view must be correct** — Being different for its own sake is contrarianism without substance
3. **You must have the courage to act on it** — Having the right non-consensus view but failing to act is the same as not having it

> "To achieve superior investment results, you have to hold nonconsensus views regarding value, and they have to be accurate. That's not easy." — The Most Important Thing

## What Is Already Priced In

### The Efficient Market Hypothesis — Marks's Nuanced View

Marks neither accepts nor rejects the efficient market hypothesis (EMH). His position:

> "I think the EMH is wrong — not in the sense that prices are always right, but in the sense that it's not totally efficient. The EMH is a useful working assumption, but it is not a correct description of reality." — Oaktree Memo

**Marks's practical position:**
- Markets are **often** efficient — most of the time, prices reflect available information reasonably well
- Markets are **not always** efficient — at extremes (bubbles and panics), prices deviate significantly from value
- The **degree of inefficiency** varies by market segment — large-cap U.S. equities are highly efficient; distressed debt, emerging markets, and small caps are less so
- Your edge must come from **being in less efficient markets** or from **recognizing the rare moments when efficient markets become temporarily inefficient**

### The "Already Priced In" Framework

Before every investment decision, ask:

1. **What does the current price imply about expectations?**
   - If a stock trades at 30x earnings, the market expects strong future growth
   - Your edge requires you to know whether actual growth will exceed or fall short of these expectations

2. **Is the information I'm using already widely known?**
   - Public financial data, analyst estimates, and news are already reflected in the price
   - Your edge must come from superior interpretation, not superior information

3. **Am I betting on direction or magnitude?**
   - First-level thinking: "Earnings will be good" → The market already knows that
   - Second-level thinking: "Earnings will be better than the market expects by 15%" → This is a potentially actionable insight

> "An accurate estimate of intrinsic value is the indispensable starting point, but it's not enough. To achieve superior results, you have to hold nonconsensus views regarding value, and they have to be accurate." — The Most Important Thing

## The Asymmetry of Being Right

### It's Not Just About Being Right — It's About Being Right When Others Are Wrong

Even accurate forecasts have no value if they're consensus forecasts — the accuracy is already in the price.

| Your View | Consensus View | Price Reflects | Your Edge |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|-----------|
| Bullish | Bullish | Bullish expectations | None — you're paying for optimism |
| Bearish | Bearish | Bearish expectations | None — the pessimism is already priced |
| Bullish | Bearish | Bearish expectations | **Potential edge** — if you're right, you bought cheap |
| Bearish | Bullish | Bullish expectations | **Potential edge** — if you're right, you sold high |

**The implication:** The best opportunities arise when:
- The market is pessimistic about something you believe will turn out okay (buy)
- The market is optimistic about something you believe has hidden risks (sell or avoid)

## Practical Application: The Second-Level Thinking Audit

For every investment thesis, answer these questions:

1. **What is the range of likely future outcomes?** (Not one outcome — a range)
2. **Which outcome do I think is most likely?** (And what is my confidence level?)
3. **What outcome does the consensus expect?** (What is priced in?)
4. **Is my view different from the consensus?** (If not, stop — no edge)
5. **If my view is different, what do I know or see that the consensus doesn't?** (This must be specific)
6. **How often has the consensus been wrong about similar situations in the past?** (Base rates matter)
7. **If I'm wrong and the consensus is right, what do I lose?** (Asymmetry check)
8. **Am I being non-consensus because of analysis, or because of ego/stubbornness?** (Self-honesty check)

> "The most important thing is knowing when you have an edge and when you don't. If you don't have an edge, you shouldn't play." — Oaktree Memo

## Second-Level Thinking in Different Contexts

### In Bull Markets
First-level: "The economy is growing, buy stocks."
Second-level: "The economy is growing, everyone is buying stocks, prices are elevated, risk premiums have compressed, expected returns going forward are therefore low. Be cautious."

### In Bear Markets
First-level: "The economy is shrinking, sell everything."
Second-level: "The economy is shrinking, everyone is panicking, prices have collapsed well below intrinsic value, risk premiums are enormous. This is when the best investments are made."

### In Specific Sectors
First-level: "AI is transforming everything. Buy AI stocks."
Second-level: "AI is transforming everything. Everyone knows this. AI stocks are priced for perfection. The question isn't whether AI will be important — it's whether AI stocks will be a good investment at these prices. What margin of safety exists if AI adoption is slower than expected?"
