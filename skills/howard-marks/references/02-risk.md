# Risk: The Most Misunderstood Concept in Investing

> When to read this file: when assessing risk in an investment, when distinguishing between risk and volatility, when evaluating whether an investment offers adequate compensation for risk, or when discussing risk management philosophy.

This is Marks's most important and most original contribution to investment thinking. His framework for understanding risk is fundamentally different from academic finance.

> "Risk means more things can happen than will happen." — Elroy Dimson, as frequently quoted by Marks

---

## Risk Is NOT Volatility

### The Academic Definition (Which Marks Rejects)

Modern portfolio theory (MPT) defines risk as volatility — the standard deviation of returns. By this definition, an asset that goes up 10% and down 10% alternately is "riskier" than one that goes up 3% every period.

**Why Marks considers this definition dangerous:**

> "I think of risk as the probability of a bad outcome, specifically the probability of permanent capital loss. Volatility is a temporary fluctuation in price. Risk is a permanent impairment of value. These are very different things." — The Most Important Thing

**The critical distinction:**
- **Volatility** is observable, measurable, and mathematical — but it describes the past
- **Risk** is forward-looking, subjective, and unquantifiable — but it describes what actually matters
- A stock that drops 50% but recovers was volatile, not risky
- A stock that drops 50% and never recovers was both volatile and risky
- **The problem:** You can't tell the difference in advance. That uncertainty IS risk.

> "The possibility of permanent loss is the risk I worry about, Oaktree worries about, and every practical investor I know worries about." — Oaktree Memo, "Risk Revisited"

### Six Dimensions of Investment Risk

Marks identifies multiple dimensions of risk that are invisible to volatility-based measures:

1. **Risk of permanent loss**: The investment goes to zero or near-zero and never recovers
2. **Risk of falling short of goals**: The return is positive but insufficient for your needs (e.g., retirement funding)
3. **Risk of missing opportunities**: Being too cautious and missing the best investments (opportunity cost)
4. **Risk of being forced to sell**: Margin calls, redemptions, or personal need for cash force selling at the worst time
5. **Risk of looking wrong in the short term**: Correct long-term positioning that underperforms in the short term, creating career risk or emotional pressure to abandon
6. **Risk of excessive certainty**: Being so confident in a thesis that you oversize the position and can't survive being wrong

> "There are many things that can go wrong in investing, and it's wrong to reduce them all to one number." — The Most Important Thing

## The Risk/Return Relationship Is NOT Linear

### The Capital Market Line Is Misleading

Academic finance teaches that higher risk produces higher returns — the "risk premium." Marks considers this one of the most dangerous beliefs in investing.

> "Riskier investments absolutely cannot be counted on to deliver higher returns. If they could, they wouldn't be riskier." — The Most Important Thing

**Marks's actual risk/return framework:**

The **expected** return on riskier investments is higher — otherwise no one would invest in them. But the **range of possible outcomes** is wider. This means:
- Best outcomes are better than with safe investments
- Worst outcomes are worse than with safe investments
- The **actual** return is uncertain and may be anywhere in the range
- In bad markets, the "risk premium" turns into a "risk penalty"

> "Higher-risk investments do offer higher expected returns, but that merely means the return if things go well will be higher. The return if things go badly can be awful." — Oaktree Memo

### The Bell Curve of Outcomes

For a safe investment (Treasury bond): narrow distribution, returns cluster tightly around the expected value.

For a risky investment (high-yield bond, stock, venture): wide distribution, returns can be far above or far below the expected value.

**The critical insight:** When you invest in high-risk assets, you're not "buying" a higher return. You're buying a **wider range of possible outcomes** — which includes both exceptional gains AND catastrophic losses.

## Risk Is Highest When Everyone Thinks It's Lowest

This is Marks's most counterintuitive and most valuable insight.

> "The greatest risk doesn't come from low quality or high volatility. It comes from paying prices that are too high. And the greatest risk of paying too high a price comes when things look best." — The Most Important Thing

**The mechanism:**
1. Good news creates optimism → prices rise
2. Rising prices create more optimism → prices rise further
3. High prices compress risk premiums → expected returns fall
4. But investors feel safe because "everything is going well"
5. Low perceived risk causes investors to take on more actual risk (more leverage, less diversification, lower quality)
6. **At the moment of maximum comfort, actual risk is highest**

The reverse is equally true:
1. Bad news creates pessimism → prices fall
2. Falling prices create more pessimism → prices fall further
3. Low prices expand risk premiums → expected returns rise
4. But investors feel terrified because "everything is falling apart"
5. High perceived risk causes investors to avoid risk entirely
6. **At the moment of maximum fear, actual risk is lowest**

> "What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end." — Warren Buffett, quoted by Marks in multiple memos

## Risk Control vs. Risk Avoidance

### A Crucial Distinction

> "Risk control is the essential ingredient in successful investing. And risk control is not the same as risk avoidance." — Oaktree Memo

**Risk avoidance** means investing only in safe assets (Treasuries, cash). This eliminates the risk of loss but also eliminates the possibility of return. Over time, it guarantees falling behind inflation.

**Risk control** means investing in risky assets but:
- Understanding the risks explicitly
- Ensuring adequate compensation for each risk
- Sizing positions so that no single mistake is fatal
- Maintaining the ability to survive adverse outcomes

**The Oaktree philosophy:**
> "We don't know what's going to happen. We don't know when it's going to happen. We just try to arrange our affairs so we'll do okay if things go well, and we'll survive if they don't." — Marks

### The Invisible Nature of Good Risk Management

> "Risk control is invisible in good times. It only becomes visible when things go wrong." — Oaktree Memo, "Risk Revisited"

A portfolio that returns 10% in a good year with risk controls looks the same as one that returns 10% without risk controls. The difference only appears in a bad year: the controlled portfolio might decline 15% while the uncontrolled one declines 40%.

**The implication:** Risk management is chronically underappreciated because its benefits are only visible in crises — which happen rarely but devastatingly.

## Practical Risk Assessment Framework

For every investment, apply Marks's risk analysis:

### Step 1: Identify All Possible Outcomes
Not just "bull case" and "bear case" — the full range from best to worst.

### Step 2: Assess Probabilities
What is the probability of each outcome? Be honest about uncertainty.

### Step 3: Check Compensation
At the current price, am I being adequately compensated for the downside risks?

### Step 4: Stress Test
What happens if:
- Revenue drops 30%?
- Credit markets freeze?
- The competitive advantage erodes?
- Management makes a bad capital allocation decision?
- Multiple bad things happen simultaneously?

### Step 5: Survivability
If the worst realistic scenario occurs:
- Can I hold the position without being forced to sell?
- Will I still have capital to invest in the opportunities that the crisis creates?
- Does this position threaten the survival of my overall portfolio?

### Step 6: The "Marks Test"
> "Does the price give me a margin for error if I'm wrong about the fundamentals?"

If you can't survive being wrong, the position is too large regardless of how right you think you are.
