# Price vs. Value and Contrarian Investing

> When to read this file: when evaluating the relationship between an asset's price and its intrinsic value, when considering contrarian positions, when analyzing whether the market is offering adequate compensation for risk, or when discussing the discipline of buying cheap and selling dear.

> "The relationship between price and value holds the ultimate key to investment success." — The Most Important Thing

---

## Price and Value Are Not the Same Thing

### The Fundamental Insight

> "For a value investor, price has to be the starting point. It has been demonstrated countless times that no asset is so good that it can't become a bad investment if bought at too high a price. And there are few assets so bad that they can't be a good investment when bought cheap enough." — The Most Important Thing

This simple idea has profound implications:
- A wonderful company at a terrible price is a terrible investment
- A mediocre company at a wonderful price can be a wonderful investment
- **Investment success is not about buying good things, but about buying things well**

> "There's no such thing as a good or bad idea regardless of price." — Oaktree Memo

### The Marks 2×2 Matrix

| | Low Price | High Price |
|---|----------|-----------|
| **Good Asset** | ✅ The best investment: quality at a bargain | ⚠️ Dangerous: quality but no margin of safety |
| **Bad Asset** | 🔍 Possibly interesting: if price is low enough | ❌ The worst investment: poor quality AND overpriced |

Most investors focus on the rows (asset quality). Marks focuses on the columns (price discipline).

## Intrinsic Value: The Anchor

### How Marks Estimates Value

Marks does not use a single valuation method. He uses multiple approaches and looks for convergence:

1. **Asset-based valuation**: What are the assets worth in liquidation or private sale?
2. **Earnings-based valuation**: What is a reasonable multiple of normalized earnings/cash flow?
3. **Comparable transactions**: What have similar assets sold for in private transactions?
4. **Replacement cost**: What would it cost to build this business from scratch?
5. **Discounted cash flow**: Present value of future cash flows (used cautiously — too many assumptions)

> "The key to a value approach is, first, determining what something is worth, and then paying substantially less for it." — Oaktree Memo

### Value Is a Range, Not a Point

> "Intrinsic value is not a precise number. I think of it as a range. The question is whether the current price is materially above or below that range." — Various Marks interviews

**Practical implication:** Don't pretend to know that a stock is worth exactly $47.32. Know that it's worth somewhere between $40 and $55, and act accordingly:
- If the price is $30, you have a margin of safety regardless of where in the range the true value falls
- If the price is $50, you have no margin of safety and could easily lose money
- If the price is $45, it depends on which end of the range you believe is most likely

## Contrarian Investing: The Courage to Be Different

### What Contrarianism Really Means

> "To buy when others are despondently selling and to sell when others are avidly buying requires the greatest fortitude and pays the greatest ultimate rewards." — Sir John Templeton (frequently quoted by Marks)

**What contrarianism is NOT:**
- Being contrarian for its own sake (that's just stubbornness)
- Always doing the opposite of the crowd (the crowd is often right)
- Ignoring consensus analysis (useful information, even if incomplete)

**What contrarianism IS:**
- Recognizing when the crowd has taken prices to an extreme
- Having the courage to buy when prices reflect excessive pessimism
- Having the discipline to sell (or not buy) when prices reflect excessive optimism
- Being willing to be early and look wrong for a while

> "The thing I find most interesting about investing is how paradoxical it is: how often the things that seem most obvious — that everyone agrees on — turn out not to be true." — The Most Important Thing

### Why Being Contrarian Is So Hard

1. **Social proof**: Humans are wired to follow the herd. Going against it creates intense psychological discomfort.
2. **Career risk**: Professional investors who underperform for 2-3 years get fired, even if they're ultimately right.
3. **Being early looks the same as being wrong**: You can buy at what you think is cheap, watch it get cheaper, and doubt yourself.
4. **The crowd IS right most of the time**: Markets are generally efficient. The contrarian opportunity only exists at extremes.

> "Skepticism and pessimism aren't synonymous. Skepticism calls for pessimism when optimism is excessive. But it also calls for optimism when pessimism is excessive." — The Most Important Thing

## Patient Opportunism: Marks's Investment Style

### The Anatomy of a Bargain

Marks describes three conditions that create bargains:

1. **Forced selling**: An investor MUST sell regardless of price (margin calls, fund redemptions, regulatory requirements). This creates artificial supply unrelated to fundamental value.

2. **Unpopularity**: An asset, sector, or country is out of favor. Investors avoid it not because of analysis but because of stigma, fatigue, or career risk.

3. **Complexity**: An asset is too complex for most investors to analyze. Those who can do the work are rewarded with higher returns.

> "The best purchases we've ever made have come from situations where others were forced to sell or didn't want to own something — not because there was anything fundamentally wrong with it." — Oaktree Memo

### The Waiting Game

> "Oaktree's motto is: 'If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves.' The way to avoid losers is to not buy when things are overpriced, even if that means sitting on cash and watching others make money for a while." — Various Marks talks

**The cost of patience:**
- Underperformance during bull markets (cash drag)
- Client complaints ("Why are we holding so much cash?")
- Self-doubt ("Am I being too cautious?")

**The reward of patience:**
- Capital available to deploy at the bottom
- Ability to buy quality assets at distressed prices
- Avoiding permanent capital loss from buying at the top

> "There's a lot of wisdom in the phrase 'this too shall pass.' It applies to good times as well as bad." — Mastering the Market Cycle

## The Marks Value Discipline in Practice

### When to Buy

✅ Buy when:
- Price is materially below your estimate of intrinsic value
- The gap exists because of **temporary** factors (sentiment, forced selling, neglect)
- You can articulate specifically why the consensus is wrong
- You have a margin of safety large enough to survive being partially wrong
- The asymmetry is favorable (upside >> downside)

### When NOT to Buy

❌ Don't buy when:
- Price is at or above intrinsic value, even if it's a "great company"
- The gap exists because of **permanent** factors (structural decline, obsolescence)
- You're buying because "everyone else is buying" or "it's gone up a lot"
- There's no margin of safety (the price assumes everything goes right)
- The asymmetry is unfavorable (downside ≥ upside)

### When to Sell

Marks is less prescriptive about selling than about buying, but offers these guidelines:
- Sell when price reaches or exceeds intrinsic value (unlike Buffett, Marks does sell)
- Sell when the original thesis is no longer valid
- Sell when better opportunities emerge (opportunity cost)
- Sell when the cycle has turned and risk/return has become unfavorable
- **Don't sell just because the price dropped** — if the thesis is intact, a lower price is a better buy
