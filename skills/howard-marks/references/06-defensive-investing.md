# Defensive Investing: Asymmetry, Avoiding Losers, and Margin of Error

> When to read this file: when evaluating whether an investment offers favorable asymmetry, when building a portfolio designed to avoid permanent loss, when considering the balance between offense and defense, or when applying Marks's tennis analogy to investment strategy.

> "The road to long-term investment success runs through risk control more than through aggressiveness." — The Most Important Thing

---

## The Tennis Analogy: Amateur vs. Professional

### Marks's Most Famous Metaphor

> "In professional tennis, the outcome is determined by the winner — the player who hits the most winning shots. In amateur tennis, the outcome is determined by the loser — the player who makes the most errors. Investing is like amateur tennis." — The Most Important Thing, citing Charles Ellis

**The implication:**
- In investing, your return is primarily determined by how many **losers you avoid**, not how many **winners you find**
- A portfolio that avoids catastrophic losses will outperform a portfolio that swings for home runs, most of the time
- **Consistency beats brilliance** — 10% per year for 20 years (6.7x) beats alternating between +40% and -20% (5.1x over the same period)

> "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves." — Oaktree Capital's unofficial motto

### Why Defense Wins

**The math of losses:**
- Lose 10% → Need +11% to recover
- Lose 25% → Need +33% to recover
- Lose 50% → Need +100% to recover
- Lose 75% → Need +300% to recover

**The asymmetry of losses means that avoiding a big loss is more valuable than capturing a big gain.** A 50% gain followed by a 33% loss leaves you at breakeven. But a 33% loss followed by a 50% gain also leaves you at breakeven. The loss hurts more than the gain helps.

## Asymmetry: The Quality of Returns

### What Marks Means by Asymmetry

> "I think what distinguishes a truly excellent investor is that they achieve high returns with controlled risk — better returns relative to risk." — Oaktree Memo

**Asymmetry** in Marks's framework means capturing a disproportionate share of the upside while experiencing a smaller share of the downside.

**Positive asymmetry (what Marks seeks):**
- When the market goes up 20%, the portfolio goes up 18% (captures most of the upside)
- When the market goes down 20%, the portfolio goes down 10% (protects against the downside)
- Over time, this asymmetry compounds into dramatically superior results

**Negative asymmetry (what Marks avoids):**
- When the market goes up 20%, the portfolio goes up 25% (captures more than the upside)
- When the market goes down 20%, the portfolio goes down 30% (amplifies the downside)
- This is what leverage does — it creates negative asymmetry

### Measuring Asymmetry

**Marks's implicit formula:**
```
Asymmetry Ratio = (Portfolio gain in up markets / Market gain) ÷ (Portfolio loss in down markets / Market loss)

Example:
  Up capture: 90% (portfolio gets 18% when market gets 20%)
  Down capture: 50% (portfolio loses 10% when market loses 20%)
  Asymmetry ratio: 0.90 / 0.50 = 1.80

  A ratio > 1.0 means positive asymmetry
  A ratio < 1.0 means negative asymmetry
```

**Oaktree's track record** exemplifies positive asymmetry: they've generally participated in most of the market's upside while experiencing significantly less of the downside — particularly in credit markets.

## Margin of Error

### Why You Need One

> "If your estimate of intrinsic value is right and you buy at that price, you'll earn a fair return. But if your estimate is wrong, or if bad luck intervenes, you can suffer a permanent loss. The solution is to insist on a margin of error — to buy only when the price is significantly below your estimate of intrinsic value." — The Most Important Thing

**Why you can't skip the margin of error:**
1. **Your estimate of value is imprecise** (it's always a range, never a point)
2. **The future is unpredictable** (unexpected events happen)
3. **You might be wrong** (overconfidence is the most common investor bias)
4. **Markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent** (Keynes)

### How Much Margin Is Enough?

Marks doesn't specify a fixed margin of safety. Instead, he adjusts it based on:

| Situation | Required Margin | Reasoning |
|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| High-quality, stable business | 10-15% | Lower uncertainty → less margin needed |
| Cyclical business | 25-35% | Higher uncertainty about normalized earnings |
| Distressed situation | 40-50% | Extreme uncertainty, binary outcomes possible |
| Emerging market / unfamiliar territory | 30-40% | Less familiarity → more things to get wrong |
| Late in the cycle | Add 10-15% to above | Risk of multiple contraction amplifies error |

## Offense vs. Defense: Knowing When to Switch

### The Marks Positioning Framework

> "Investing is not about making the absolute best return. It's about getting the best risk-adjusted return for the environment you're in." — Oaktree Memo

**When to Play Offense (Deploy Capital Aggressively):**
- Cycle position: Bottom / early recovery
- Sentiment: Fear / panic
- Valuations: Cheap relative to history
- Credit: Tight (creating forced selling and bargains)
- Asymmetry: Highly favorable (lots of upside, limited downside at current prices)

**When to Play Defense (Preserve Capital):**
- Cycle position: Late expansion / peak
- Sentiment: Euphoria / complacency
- Valuations: Rich relative to history
- Credit: Loose / reckless
- Asymmetry: Unfavorable (limited upside, significant downside)

> "You can't predict. You can prepare." — Mastering the Market Cycle

### Marks's "Offense/Defense Dial"

Instead of binary "risk on / risk off," Marks uses a continuum:

```
← Maximum Defense ─────── Neutral ─────── Maximum Offense →
   100% cash/quality        Normal mix          Fully deployed
   Avoid risk               Selective risk       Embrace risk
   "Too expensive"          "Fair"              "Cheap"
```

**The dial should NEVER be at either extreme permanently** — that would imply certainty about the future, which Marks explicitly rejects.

**Where to set the dial depends on:**
- Where we are in the cycle
- Current valuations relative to history
- The prevailing attitude toward risk
- The availability and terms of credit

## Consistency Over Brilliance

> "The hallmark of good investors is not a few spectacular coups. It's consistently good risk-adjusted returns over the long term." — Oaktree Memo

**Marks's hierarchy of investor quality:**

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Poor** | Loses money in both good and bad times | Reckless, no process |
| **Average** | Makes money in good times, loses in bad | Most investors |
| **Good** | Makes money in good times, loses less in bad | Decent risk control |
| **Excellent** | Makes good money in good times, loses significantly less in bad | Oaktree's goal |
| **Exceptional** | Makes money in bad times too (turns crisis into opportunity) | Rare and not sustainable |

**Marks targets "Excellent"** — not "Exceptional," which he considers unsustainable and likely driven by luck.
