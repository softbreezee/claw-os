## Credit Analysis Fundamentals

### Why Credit Is Different from Equity

| Dimension | Equity | Credit |
|-----------|--------|--------|
| Upside | Unlimited | Capped (coupon + par) |
| Downside | 100% loss | 100% loss |
| Asymmetry | Symmetric | Negatively asymmetric |
| Primary concern | Growth potential | Default risk |
| Decision | "How much can I make?" | "Can I get my money back?" |
| Analytical focus | Earnings growth, moats, valuation | Cash flow adequacy, leverage, covenants |

> "Bond investing is a negative art. You succeed not by finding great bonds but by avoiding bad ones. Every bond either pays as promised or it doesn't. You succeed by avoiding the ones that don't." — Oaktree Memo

### The Five Questions of Credit Analysis

For any credit instrument:

1. **Can the borrower generate enough cash flow to service the debt?**
   - Interest coverage ratio (EBITDA/Interest) > 2x minimum
   - Free cash flow after capex sufficient for debt service
   - Revenue stability (cyclical businesses need higher coverage)

2. **What happens if cash flow falls short?**
   - How much can revenue/EBITDA decline before debt service is threatened?
   - What is the "breakeven" decline percentage?
   - Are there cost-cutting levers available?

3. **What is the collateral/recovery value?**
   - If the borrower defaults, what can creditors recover?
   - Asset values in liquidation vs. going-concern basis
   - Seniority of the claim (secured → senior unsecured → subordinated → equity)

4. **What are the covenants, and do they provide real protection?**
   - Leverage maintenance covenants (max Debt/EBITDA)
   - Interest coverage covenants (min EBITDA/Interest)
   - Restricted payments (limits on dividends, asset sales)
   - **"Covenant-lite" = danger** — no guardrails on borrower behavior

5. **What is the maturity profile?**
   - Can the borrower refinance at maturity?
   - What is the credit market likely to look like at maturity?
   - "Maturity wall" risk — large amounts maturing in a short period

## Capital Structure Priority

### The Waterfall

In distress or bankruptcy, recovery follows the capital structure from top to bottom:

```
1. Secured Debt (banks, first lien)        ← Highest recovery (typically 60-90%)
2. Senior Unsecured Debt (bonds)            ← Moderate recovery (typically 30-60%)
3. Subordinated Debt (sub bonds, mezzanine) ← Low recovery (typically 10-30%)
4. Preferred Equity                         ← Very low recovery (typically 0-10%)
5. Common Equity                            ← Usually zero
```

**Marks's insight:** The best risk-adjusted investments in distressed situations are often in the **fulcrum security** — the level of the capital structure where the value "breaks." Above the fulcrum, you get full recovery but limited upside. Below the fulcrum, you likely get nothing. AT the fulcrum, you might get equity in the restructured company — with significant upside if the business recovers.

> "The fulcrum security is where the action is in distressed investing. It's where bonds convert to equity and where the greatest mispricings occur." — Oaktree presentations

## Distressed Investing: Oaktree's Approach

### When Distressed Opportunities Arise

Distressed investing opportunities are **cyclical** — they emerge during credit contractions and disappear during credit expansions.

**The distressed cycle:**
1. Credit expansion → standards loosen → marginal borrowers get funded
2. Economic weakness → marginal borrowers struggle → defaults begin
3. Defaults create panic → credit markets freeze → even healthy borrowers are affected
4. Panic selling drives prices well below recovery value → **opportunity**
5. Patient capital buys distressed assets → holds through restructuring → earns outsized returns
6. Economy recovers → credit markets reopen → cycle begins again

### Cyclical vs. Structural Distress

| Type | Cyclical Distress | Structural Distress |
|------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Cause | Temporary economic weakness or credit market closure | Business model permanently broken |
| Prognosis | Company will recover when conditions normalize | Company may never recover |
| Investment thesis | Buy at distressed price, earn recovery return | Only buy if liquidation value exceeds price |
| Examples | Hotel chain during COVID, energy co during oil crash | Print newspaper, coal company in clean energy transition |
| Expected return | 2-3x over 2-4 years | 0-1x (often total loss) |

> "The most important distinction in distressed investing is between a good company with a bad balance sheet and a bad company with a bad balance sheet." — Marks, various talks

### Marks's Distressed Investing Checklist

- [ ] Is the distress cyclical (temporary) or structural (permanent)?
- [ ] What is the recovery value in a reasonable restructuring scenario?
- [ ] Where does my claim sit in the capital structure?
- [ ] Am I buying at a price that implies a recovery rate below what I estimate?
- [ ] Can I hold through a 2-3 year restructuring process?
- [ ] Do I have the legal expertise (or access to it) to navigate bankruptcy proceedings?
- [ ] Is there a "fulcrum security" that offers asymmetric upside?
- [ ] What is the catalyst for value realization? (Restructuring plan, asset sale, re-IPO)
- [ ] Are other distressed investors competing for the same position? (Crowding reduces returns)
- [ ] Is the management team competent enough to execute a turnaround?

## The High-Yield Market

### Marks's Preferred Hunting Ground

The high-yield bond market (bonds rated below investment-grade, BB+ and below) is where Marks built his career:

> "High-yield bonds are perhaps the best example of the relationship between risk and return. They offer higher yields because they have higher default risk. The question is always whether the yield adequately compensates for the risk." — Oaktree Memo

**Key metrics for high-yield analysis:**
- **Yield spread over Treasuries**: The compensation for taking credit risk. Historical average ~400-500bp for the broad index.
- **Default rate**: Historical average ~3-4% for high-yield. Spikes to 10%+ in recessions.
- **Recovery rate**: Average ~40% of par in default. Varies enormously by seniority and industry.

**When to invest in high yield:**
- Spreads significantly above historical average (>600bp) → compensation exceeds expected losses
- Default rate has already risen (losses are behind, not ahead)
- Investor sentiment is fearful (prices are depressed)

**When to avoid high yield:**
- Spreads at or below historical average (<350bp) → insufficient compensation
- Default rate is very low (suggests loose standards and future problems)
- Investor sentiment is complacent ("reaching for yield")

## Applying Credit Thinking to Equity Investing

Marks's credit background provides unique insights applicable to all investing:

1. **Think about downside first**: In credit, you start with "Can I get my money back?" Apply this to equity: "What is the floor value if things go wrong?"

2. **Leverage amplifies everything**: A company with 5x debt/EBITDA is not 5x better than one with 1x — it's 5x more fragile. Always adjust your assessment for leverage.

3. **Covenants matter**: Whether investing in equity or credit, understand what constraints exist on management behavior. Companies with no constraints on leverage, dividends, or related-party transactions are dangerous.

4. **Maturity risk is real**: A company might be perfectly healthy today but face a refinancing wall in 18 months. If credit markets are tight at that point, the equity could go to zero even though the business is fine.

5. **Recovery analysis as a floor**: Even for equity investments, estimate what the assets would be worth in a worst-case liquidation. This provides a "floor" on your loss — or confirms that there is no floor.

> "Everything I learned about investing, I learned in the bond market. The discipline of thinking about downside before upside, of insisting on adequate compensation for risk, and of recognizing that the upside is limited while the downside can be total — these lessons apply to all investing." — Marks
