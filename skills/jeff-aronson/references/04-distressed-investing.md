# Reference 04 · Distressed Investing: Restructuring Mechanics and the Bankruptcy Timeline

> "Bankruptcy is not the end of a company — it's a legal process for redistributing ownership. The business continues; only the claims change. Understanding the process is understanding where the value goes."
> — Distressed investing principle

## The Distressed Investing Universe

Distressed investing is not a single strategy. It encompasses a spectrum from "stressed" credits trading at 80 cents to true liquidations at pennies on the dollar. Understanding where a company sits on this spectrum determines the appropriate analytical framework and instrument.

**The Distressed Spectrum**:

| Category | YTW Range | Dollar Price | Situation | Primary Return Driver |
|----------|----------|-------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Stressed / High Beta | 8–12% | 70–85 cents | Credit concerns, potential covenant breach | Trading recovery to par; spread compression |
| Distressed | 12–20% | 40–70 cents | Likely restructuring within 12–24 months | Recovery in restructuring; fulcrum to equity |
| Defaulted | N/A | 10–50 cents | Post-default; in or near bankruptcy | Recovery analysis; plan of reorganization |
| Deeply Distressed | N/A | <20 cents | Liquidation likely | Asset recovery; DIP financing |

## Pre-Bankruptcy: The Out-of-Court Restructuring

Most distressed situations are resolved **without** filing for bankruptcy protection. Out-of-court restructurings are faster, cheaper, less disruptive to the business, and avoid the stigma of bankruptcy — which matters enormously for customer and employee retention.

### Exchange Offers

The company offers existing bondholders the opportunity to exchange their current bonds for new securities:
- New bonds with lower coupon, extended maturity, or reduced principal
- New equity (a debt-for-equity swap)
- A combination of cash + new bonds + new equity

**Why holders accept**: The alternative is likely a worse outcome in formal bankruptcy.
**Why holders refuse**: Holdout problem — if most holders tender but you don't, you're still owed the original terms while others took the haircut. Exchange offers often fail because of holdout behavior.

**Minimum participation threshold**: Most exchange offers require 90%+ participation to be effective. Below that threshold, the holdouts remain with original claims and can potentially destroy the restructuring by pursuing legal remedies.

### Consent Solicitations

The company solicits bondholder consent to amend the bond indenture, removing covenants or extending maturities without exchanging the instrument. Requires typically 50–66⅔% of outstanding principal.

### Amend-and-Extend (A&E)

Bank lenders agree to extend the maturity of term loans in exchange for higher pricing, tighter covenants, or equity warrants. Simpler than a full exchange offer; lenders are often willing because the alternative (forcing the company into bankruptcy) is worse for them too.

## Chapter 11 Bankruptcy: The Legal Framework

When out-of-court solutions fail, the company files for Chapter 11 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code — "reorganization" as opposed to Chapter 7 ("liquidation").

### Filing Day: The Automatic Stay

Upon filing, the **automatic stay** immediately halts:
- All collection actions by creditors
- Enforcement of contracts (suppliers, landlords cannot terminate)
- Foreclosure on assets
- All lawsuits against the debtor

The stay gives the company breathing room to reorganize without the chaos of every creditor racing to seize assets simultaneously.

### The Debtor-in-Possession (DIP)

The existing management typically remains in place as the **debtor-in-possession (DIP)** — running the company during the bankruptcy process. The court, creditors' committee, and eventually the bankruptcy judge provide oversight.

**Why this matters**: Management continuity preserves operational value. If management is the problem (fraud, gross incompetence), the court can appoint a trustee instead, but this is relatively rare.

## DIP Financing: The First Major Opportunity

### What Is DIP Financing?

**DIP (Debtor-in-Possession) financing** is new credit extended to a company after it files for Chapter 11. DIP loans receive **super-priority status** — they are repaid before any other pre-petition creditor, ahead of even the first lien secured lenders.

DIP financing is essential because:
- The company needs cash to continue operations during bankruptcy
- Existing suppliers may demand cash on delivery rather than extending credit
- Employee wages, rents, and operating costs must be paid
- Pre-petition lenders may be unwilling to extend additional credit (they're now creditors in the bankruptcy)

### Why DIP Financing Is Attractive for Distressed Investors

1. **Super-priority repayment**: DIP lenders are first in line for repayment — ahead of all pre-petition creditors — reducing credit risk substantially
2. **Premium pricing**: DIP loans carry significantly higher rates than regular secured lending (SOFR + 600–1,000 bps is common, plus upfront fees of 2–4%)
3. **Lender control**: DIP lenders often include milestones in the DIP agreement — sale process deadlines, plan filing dates — that control the restructuring timeline
4. **Access to information**: As DIP lender, you have complete visibility into the company's operations, finances, and restructuring process — superior information compared to other creditors

### DIP Roll-Up

Often, pre-petition first lien lenders serve as DIP lenders, and the DIP facility "rolls up" the existing pre-petition first lien debt into the super-priority DIP. This benefits them dramatically: their pre-petition claims get re-characterized as super-priority DIP claims, leaping over other creditors.

**For a distressed investor**: If you hold pre-petition first lien debt, aggressively push to be included in the DIP roll-up. If you are entering the situation fresh, providing DIP financing gives you the highest-priority claim and operational control.

## Section 363 Sales

### What Is a 363 Sale?

Under Section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code, a company can sell assets "free and clear" of all liens, claims, and encumbrances — meaning the buyer receives clean title regardless of what claims encumber the asset in bankruptcy.

363 sales have become increasingly common as a restructuring mechanism because they:
- Move faster than a full plan of reorganization (can complete in 60–90 days vs. 12–18 months for a plan)
- Transfer assets cleanly without successor liability
- Create competitive bidding that may maximize value

### The Stalking Horse Bid

A **stalking horse bid** is a pre-negotiated offer to purchase the assets, submitted before the formal auction process. The stalking horse:
- Sets a floor price for the auction
- Receives breakup fee protection (typically 2–4% of the purchase price) if outbid
- Gains the right to "credit bid" (see below)
- Obtains complete due diligence access before the auction

**Why be the stalking horse?** The stalking horse gets the best information access, shapes the auction process (bid procedures, timeline), and has a significant advantage in the auction itself. The breakup fee provides partial compensation if outbid. For Centerbridge-style investors, being the stalking horse is a way to control the restructuring outcome.

### Credit Bidding

**Credit bidding** is the right of a secured creditor to bid for assets using the face value of their secured claim rather than cash. If you hold $500M in first lien debt and the auction maximum bid is $600M, you can bid $500M of debt + $100M cash rather than $600M cash.

Credit bidding is enormously powerful:
- It gives secured creditors a built-in advantage over purely cash bidders
- It can effectively lock out competing bidders who need to finance an all-cash purchase
- It is the primary mechanism by which lenders become owners in 363 sales

**Aronson's insight**: Accumulating first lien debt in a distressed company is not just a credit investment — it's an option to become the owner through credit bidding in a 363 sale.

## Plan of Reorganization (POR)

### What Is a Plan of Reorganization?

The Plan of Reorganization (POR) is the comprehensive legal document that describes how all claims will be treated in Chapter 11:
- Which creditors get paid in full (in cash or new debt)
- Which creditors receive partial recovery (in cash, new debt, or equity)
- Which creditors receive nothing
- How the reorganized company will be capitalized
- Who will own the reorganized company

### Classes and Voting

Claims are organized into **classes** (e.g., first lien claims, second lien claims, senior unsecured, equity). Each class votes separately on the plan:
- **Accepting**: ≥ 50% by number and ≥ 66⅔% by dollar value of voting claimants
- **Rejecting**: Below the acceptance threshold
- A **deemed rejecting class** (e.g., equity receiving nothing) is presumed to reject

### Cramdown

A plan can be **confirmed over the objection of dissenting classes** ("crammed down") if:
1. At least one impaired class has accepted the plan
2. The plan does not discriminate unfairly among classes
3. The plan is "fair and equitable" — for secured creditors this means receiving the value of their collateral; for unsecured, it means no junior class receives anything unless the dissenting class is paid in full

Cramdown is the nuclear option — it allows the fulcrum security holders (who typically craft the plan) to force their preferred restructuring on dissenting junior classes.

### Equitization of Debt

The most common POR mechanism in large Chapter 11 cases: **unsecured bondholders (the fulcrum) receive equity in the reorganized company in exchange for cancellation of their debt claims.** This is sometimes called a "debt-for-equity swap."

The reorganized company emerges from bankruptcy with:
- A significantly reduced debt load (the equitized debt is gone)
- New equity holders (the former bondholders)
- Fresh start accounting (assets marked to fair value)
- An operating business that can now service its remaining debt

**The reorganized equity's value** = (Reorganized company's enterprise value) − (Remaining debt post-emergence)

If bondholders receive 100% of reorganized equity and the reorganized company is worth $2B with $500M of remaining debt, the reorganized equity is worth $1.5B. If the original fulcrum bonds had face value of $800M and the company issued $1.5B in equity to those bondholders, each bondholder receives $1.50 in equity for every $1.00 of face value — a premium to their pre-bankruptcy trading price of (say) 70 cents.

## The Distressed Timeline

Understanding the timeline is essential for managing liquidity and return expectations:

```
Event                               Typical Timeline from Stress Onset
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Credit spreads widen significantly    Month 0–3 (opportunity entry point)
Covenant breach / waiver request      Month 3–9
Out-of-court exchange offer attempt   Month 6–12 (often fails)
Chapter 11 filing                     Month 9–18
DIP financing arranged                Filing + 1–5 days
First day motions                     Filing + 1–3 days
Bar date (deadline for filing claims) Filing + 90–180 days
Exclusivity period (debtor has        Filing + 120 days (extendable)
  exclusive right to propose plan)
Plan confirmed                        Filing + 9–18 months (typical)
Emergence from bankruptcy             Confirmation + 30–90 days
Post-emergence equity begins trading  Emergence + 1–30 days
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Total elapsed time (filing to exit):  12–24 months (typical)
Fast-track pre-packaged bankruptcy:   30–90 days filing to emergence
Complex multi-party litigation:       3–7+ years
```

### Pre-Packaged Bankruptcy

A **pre-packaged bankruptcy** ("prepack") is a restructuring where the company negotiates the POR with key creditor classes *before* filing. The plan is essentially agreed upon before the Chapter 11 case begins.

- **Speed**: Prepack can emerge from bankruptcy in 30–60 days vs. 12–18 months for a traditional Chapter 11
- **Requirements**: Need agreement from the requisite voting classes before filing (66⅔% by dollar, 50% by number of unsecured bondholders; 100% of secured classes ideally)
- **CIT Group example**: Filed November 1, 2009; emerged December 10, 2009 — 39 days total

Prepacks minimize operational disruption and time-value-of-money costs. The tradeoff is that you need to negotiate the plan with bondholders before filing — which requires disclosure of material non-public information to all negotiating parties.

## Key Concepts at a Glance

| Concept | Definition | Why It Matters |
|---------|-----------|----------------|
| DIP financing | Super-priority new debt to fund operations during bankruptcy | Highest seniority; best risk-adjusted instrument in distressed |
| 363 sale | Asset sale "free and clear" of all claims; requires court approval | Fastest value realization; stalking horse controls the outcome |
| Credit bidding | Secured creditor bids using face value of their claim | Turns debt position into equity ownership |
| Stalking horse | Pre-negotiated first bidder in 363 auction | Best information access; breakup fee protection |
| POR | Plan distributing value to all claim classes | The legal mechanism for debt-to-equity conversion |
| Cramdown | Confirming plan over dissenting class objection | Fulcrum holders can force plan on junior classes |
| Equitization | Creditors receive equity in lieu of debt repayment | Primary mechanism for fulcrum conversion to equity |
| Absolute priority | Senior claims paid before junior claims in full | Legal rule; frequently negotiated around in practice |
| Pre-packaged BK | POR negotiated before filing; 30–90 day process | Best of both worlds: legal protection + speed |
| Exclusivity | Debtor has exclusive right to file plan for 120 days | Critical period — creditors fight for/against exclusivity |
