# Reference 06 · The Solution Provider Framework: "Be the Call, Not the Put"

> "In distressed investing, you can be the person who profits from a problem or the person who solves a problem. We'd rather solve it. The returns are better and the relationships last."
> — Jeff Aronson, Centerbridge Partners

## The Core Philosophy: Call vs. Put

In options terminology:
- **Owning a put**: You profit when the underlying deteriorates. Your best outcome is bad news for the issuer.
- **Owning a call**: You profit when the underlying improves. Your best outcome is good news for the issuer.

**Traditional distressed investing** is often a put: buy cheap bonds and hope the company either recovers (you profit) or goes bankrupt quickly and you get a good recovery (you profit, but the company fails). The investor is essentially betting on outcomes they don't control and may not have aligned interests.

**The Centerbridge solution provider model** is a call: **proactively offer capital and solutions before the crisis becomes a bankruptcy.** You profit because the company survives and thrives. Your interests are fully aligned with the company's success. You are not betting against the company — you are betting on it, with the protection of a senior instrument and governance rights.

The distinction has profound implications:
- **Reputation**: Companies seek out Centerbridge proactively because Centerbridge has a track record of solving problems, not profiting from failures
- **Access**: Companies share sensitive information with Centerbridge early (before a public crisis) because they trust Centerbridge not to exploit the information
- **Returns**: Solutions provided before formal distress are typically better priced than securities purchased in a chaotic bankruptcy auction
- **Competition**: Few investors can execute complex structured solutions; there is no auction for rescue financing the way there is an auction for distressed bonds

> "When a company calls us with a problem, we want them calling us because they know we'll help them solve it, not because they have no choice. That's the difference between a relationship and a transaction."

## The Rescue Financing Framework

### When Traditional Sources Won't Lend

Rescue financing opportunities arise when a company has capital needs that no traditional lender can or will meet:

**Situation 1: Banks have pulled back**
- The company's bank revolver is being reduced or not renewed
- Leveraged loan market is closed to this credit rating
- No new bank is willing to underwrite the risk
- **Centerbridge opportunity**: Provide first lien secured rescue financing at premium pricing, with full collateral package

**Situation 2: HY bond market is shut**
- The company's HY bonds mature in 18 months but the market is in risk-off mode
- The company cannot refinance at any reasonable rate
- Existing bondholders are fearful but not organized
- **Centerbridge opportunity**: Provide a bridge facility (short-term high-yield debt) at premium pricing that buys time; simultaneously negotiate with existing bondholders for an extension/exchange

**Situation 3: Company needs equity but doesn't want to sell control**
- Operating cash flow is insufficient to fund growth capex or an acquisition
- Traditional bank debt would breach existing covenants
- PE firms want too much equity control
- **Centerbridge opportunity**: Provide structured preferred equity at the holdco level with PIK dividend, governance rights, and conversion option (see Reference 05)

**Situation 4: Distressed but solvent**
- The company can service its current debt but cannot refinance upcoming maturities
- Existing lenders want out; new lenders demand excessive pricing
- Technical covenant breach is imminent
- **Centerbridge opportunity**: DIP-style rescue facility that addresses the maturity, extends runways, and positions Centerbridge as the key creditor through the resolution

### Rescue Financing Pricing

Rescue financing commands a significant premium over normal market rates because:
1. **Urgency**: The company cannot wait; pricing power accrues to the willing provider
2. **Risk**: The credit risk is genuinely elevated; premium reflects real risk
3. **Scarcity**: Few investors have the capital, expertise, and risk tolerance to execute rescue financing
4. **Governance**: Rescue financing comes with governance rights that have value beyond the interest rate

**Typical rescue financing pricing**:
- Cash interest: SOFR + 700–1,200 bps (significantly above normal leveraged lending)
- PIK toggle: Cash option or PIK at +50–100 bps above the cash rate
- Upfront fees: 2–5% of committed amount
- Exit fees: 1–3% on repayment
- Warrants / equity kickers: 5–15% equity stake at a specified valuation
- Total all-in IRR target: 15–25%+ depending on credit risk

## The Reputation as Competitive Advantage

### How Reputation Compounds

In rescue financing and solution-provider investing, reputation is not just a nice-to-have — it is the primary barrier to entry and the primary source of deal flow.

**The reinforcing cycle**:
1. Centerbridge solves a difficult problem for Company X
2. Company X's management, board, and advisors (lawyers, bankers) observe the execution
3. When Company Y faces a similar problem, management, lawyers, and bankers *refer* Centerbridge
4. Company Y calls Centerbridge before other investors, at earlier stages, with more information
5. Centerbridge solves Company Y's problem
6. The cycle repeats; the network compounds

**What reputation specifically means in practice**:
- **Early access**: Companies call Centerbridge at Month 3 of their stress, not Month 15 (when everyone knows and the situation is chaotic)
- **Information advantage**: Companies share sensitive projections and internal analysis with Centerbridge that they wouldn't share with a distressed hedge fund that might short the stock or bonds
- **Preferred terms**: Companies accept governance provisions and pricing from Centerbridge that they would reject from an adversarial investor
- **Speed**: "We've worked with Centerbridge before — we trust them" dramatically shortens due diligence and negotiation timelines

### Maintaining the Reputation

The reputation for being a solution provider is fragile and must be actively maintained:
- **Never opportunistically exploit a situation** that was shared in confidence (even if not legally prohibited)
- **Do what you say you'll do**: If Centerbridge commits to provide capital, it provides it — even if conditions deteriorate between signing and funding
- **Leave value for the company**: Rescue financing is well-compensated; it does not need to be predatory. Pricing that extracts all upside for Centerbridge and leaves management/founders with nothing destroys reputation
- **Be honest about what you can and cannot do**: Companies value partners who tell the truth, including "this situation is too complex for us" when appropriate

> "Our reputation is worth more than any single transaction. The day we extract the last dollar from a desperate company and leave nothing for anyone else is the day the phone stops ringing."

## Case Study: CIT Group Restructuring (2009)

### Background

CIT Group was one of the largest commercial lenders in the United States, with over $75 billion in assets and approximately one million small and mid-sized business customers. The company funded itself primarily through commercial paper and medium-term notes (MTNs) — a funding model that collapsed during the 2008 financial crisis when the commercial paper market froze.

By mid-2009, CIT faced a funding cliff: billions in MTNs were maturing, the commercial paper market was closed, FDIC refused to provide guarantees, and the U.S. Treasury's TARP program had provided only $2.3 billion (insufficient). CIT's stock fell below $1. Bankruptcy was widely considered inevitable.

### The Problem

The fundamental challenge: CIT's **assets** (a performing $60B+ commercial loan portfolio) were worth substantially more than its **liabilities** in a going-concern scenario. But without funding, the assets would have to be sold in a distressed fire sale — and the fire sale value was much lower.

The question was not: "Is CIT worth saving?" The question was: "Who can execute the solution fast enough and with enough confidence to prevent a disorderly collapse?"

### The Centerbridge Approach (Dual Lens)

**Credit lens**: CIT's bonds were trading at 60–65 cents on the dollar. The implied recovery at those prices: creditors expected partial payment. But if CIT could be stabilized and funded through the crisis, the performing loan portfolio would generate sufficient cash to repay creditors at or near par. The credit was trading at a discount to its going-concern value.

**Equity lens**: CIT's franchise — relationships with one million small businesses, origination infrastructure, sector expertise (retail finance, trade finance, aerospace finance) — had genuine strategic value. A reorganized CIT with a clean balance sheet would be a valuable asset.

**Synthesis**: This was an excellent franchise with a broken funding structure, not a failed business. The solution was to provide a bridge to a restructuring that preserved the franchise while resetting the capital structure.

### The Solution

Centerbridge (along with other large investors including Baupost, Fairholme, and Klarman's hedge fund) provided a **$3 billion emergency rescue financing package** to CIT in July 2009:
- **Structure**: 2.5-year senior secured notes at 13% cash pay
- **Collateral**: First lien on all CIT assets
- **Governance**: Rights to negotiate restructuring terms; representation in any bankruptcy process
- **Fee**: 5% commitment fee on the $3 billion

This $3 billion bought CIT time to negotiate a pre-packaged bankruptcy with its bondholders — a process that ultimately took only five months.

### The Restructuring Outcome

CIT filed Chapter 11 on November 1, 2009 — with a pre-packaged plan already negotiated. The plan:
- Existing senior bondholders received equity in reorganized CIT (debt-for-equity swap)
- Existing equity was wiped out
- CIT emerged from bankruptcy on December 10, 2009 — **39 days after filing**

The emergency rescue financing providers were repaid in full (plus the 13% interest and 5% fee) from the reorganized company's proceeds. The reorganized equity received by former bondholders subsequently appreciated substantially. CIT was eventually acquired by First Citizens BancShares in 2022.

**Returns**: The emergency rescue financing earned ~18–20% IRR (13% cash interest + 5% fee amortized over the ~6 month holding period). Investors who also held CIT bonds that converted to reorganized equity earned significantly more over the subsequent years.

### Lessons from CIT

1. **Speed matters**: The $3B rescue financing was negotiated in days. Centerbridge's ability to move fast — deploying capital quickly with confidence — was the decisive competitive advantage.

2. **Solution provider positioned Centerbridge as the orchestrator**: By providing the rescue financing, Centerbridge obtained information rights, negotiating rights, and creditor standing that allowed it to help shape the pre-packaged plan.

3. **The solution was the entry**: Rather than buying CIT bonds in the open market (chasing a liquid security), Centerbridge became a direct lender — accessing superior terms, superior seniority, and superior governance.

4. **Reputation enabled the opportunity**: CIT's advisors (Skadden Arps, restructuring bankers) called Centerbridge because Centerbridge had done this before and was known to be able to execute. A smaller or less experienced firm would not have received the call.

> "CIT was in some ways the proof of concept for everything we had been building at Centerbridge. We weren't a bondholder waiting to see what happened. We were the people making it happen."

## Identifying Solution Provider Opportunities

### The "Solution Needed" Checklist

A situation is ripe for a solution-provider approach (rather than secondary bond purchase) when:

| Criterion | Description | Yes/No |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **Private access** | Can Centerbridge get direct access to management and full financials? | |
| **Timing** | Is the company facing a definitive capital event within 12–24 months? | |
| **Asset quality** | Are the underlying assets/business fundamentally sound? | |
| **Exclusivity** | Can Centerbridge structure a proprietary solution without auction? | |
| **Advisor relationship** | Does Centerbridge know the company's lawyers or bankers? | |
| **Size** | Is the transaction large enough for Centerbridge's return hurdles? | |
| **No PE overlap** | Are there no PE funds with existing control that would block Centerbridge? | |

The most attractive solution-provider opportunities check all seven boxes. The solution is proprietary, executed with information advantages, and priced at rescue financing premiums rather than market prices.

### When NOT to Be a Solution Provider

Being the solution provider is not always optimal. Sometimes it's better to be a secondary buyer of distressed bonds:

- **When the situation is already public and chaotic**: Once the situation is in the Wall Street Journal, the solution-provider window has likely closed. Buy bonds in the secondary market and participate in the restructuring as a bondholder.
- **When the company genuinely needs to restructure** (not just refinance): Chapter 11 is sometimes the right answer. Providing rescue financing to a company that should be restructuring just delays the inevitable and burns capital.
- **When existing creditors are adversarial**: If the existing first lien group is aggressive, any rescue financing Centerbridge provides will be subordinated to an entrenched adversary. Better to buy the existing senior debt at a discount.
- **When returns don't justify complexity**: Rescue financing is operationally intensive. If the return premium over simply buying secondary bonds is insufficient, do the simpler trade.
