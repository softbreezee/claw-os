# Reference 07 · Risk Management: Hedging, Sizing, and Liquidity in Distressed Strategies

> "In distressed investing, you're paid for taking on illiquidity, complexity, and legal risk — not for taking on pure beta. If you don't hedge the beta, you're just a distressed investor with an index fund problem."
> — Risk management principle

---

## Hedging Credit Positions

### The Rationale for Hedging

Distressed and structured credit portfolios generate returns from three sources:
1. **Idiosyncratic alpha**: Company-specific restructuring, operational recovery, fulcrum conversion
2. **Credit spread premium**: Compensation for assuming default risk relative to risk-free rates
3. **Beta exposure**: Correlation with broad credit markets

The **idiosyncratic alpha** is the return Centerbridge seeks. The **beta exposure** is a byproduct that can be hedged away. Without hedging, a distressed portfolio that generates +15% alpha in company turnarounds can still lose money in a systemic credit selloff.

### Credit Default Swaps (CDS)

CDS are the primary instrument for hedging single-name credit exposure:
- **Single-name CDS**: Pays off if the reference entity defaults
- **Cost**: Annual premium (CDS spread) expressed in basis points of notional

**Hedging a distressed bond position with CDS**:
- Long $10M face amount of Company X bonds at 70 cents ($7M purchase)
- Buy $10M notional of Company X CDS at 800 bps
- In a default: CDS pays off, recovering face value; bond recovery is received
- In a recovery: CDS premium paid reduces bond returns but hedges downside

**When to use single-name CDS**:
- Available and liquid for the specific issuer (HY bonds, larger issuers)
- High conviction that default is possible but want to isolate idiosyncratic risk from systematic credit widening
- Legal availability (CDS contracts are OTC; requires counterparty)

### CDS Indices (CDX, iTraxx)

For portfolio-level hedging, **CDS indices** are more efficient than single-name CDS:
- **CDX HY**: Basket of 100 U.S. high-yield issuers (5-year maturity)
- **CDX IG**: Basket of 125 U.S. investment-grade issuers
- **iTraxx**: European equivalent

**Hedging strategy**: If Centerbridge has a $100M portfolio of distressed debt with estimated 0.6 correlation to HY credit spreads, short $60M notional of CDX HY to hedge the portfolio beta.

**Costs and benefits**:
- Benefit: Hedges systemic credit widening without perfectly hedging idiosyncratic moves
- Cost: Pay the index spread each quarter
- Risk: Basis risk — the specific distress positions may not move with the index

### Interest Rate Swaps

Interest rate exposure matters for distressed portfolios in two ways:
1. **Long-duration distressed bonds**: If rates rise, all fixed-rate bonds decline in value (duration risk)
2. **Libor/SOFR resets**: Floating-rate secured debt resets with reference rates; rising rates increase interest expense on distressed companies with floating-rate debt

**Rate hedging instruments**:
- **Pay fixed / receive floating**: Hedge against rising rates (reduces mark-to-market risk on long-duration bonds)
- **Forward rate agreements (FRAs)**: Lock in future interest rates
- **Treasury futures**: Hedge duration exposure

**Practical application**: If Centerbridge owns long-duration distressed high-yield bonds and the Fed is signaling rate hikes, enter into a pay-fixed interest rate swap to hedge duration risk. The hedge reduces mark-to-market volatility even if the distressed bond is unlikely to be held to par maturity.

## Correlation with Macro: Credit in Crises

### The Credit-Beta Problem

During systemic crises, credit correlations converge to 1. Everything sells off together regardless of company-specific fundamentals. The March 2020 COVID crash is the recent example — even performing leveraged loans fell 15–20% in weeks.

**Implications for distressed investors**:
- The idiosyncratic alpha generated by company turnarounds can be swamped by systematic beta
- Liquidity disappears in crisis moments — bid-offer spreads widen, and distressed bonds become unsellable
- Even "correctly valued" positions mark down because the market is pricing in systemic fear

**Centerbridge's approach**: Maintain systematic macro hedges (CDX HY shorts, rate hedges) that are continuously in place. The cost is drag during normal times (paying CDS premia), but the protection during crises preserves capital and enables buying opportunities.

### The Illiquidity Premium vs. Duration Tradeoff

Distressed investing requires accepting illiquidity — positions cannot be exited quickly. The question is: **how much illiquidity premium should a distressed investor demand?**

**The illiquidity premium formula**:
Required Illiquidity Premium = (Expected Annual Return) − (Liquid Comparable Return) − (Risk of Forced Sale at Loss × Cost of Forced Sale)

Example:
- Distressed bond: Expected 20% IRR over 3 years
- Comparable liquid bond: 8% annual return
- Risk of forced sale: 15% (you might need liquidity in 3 years)
- Cost of forced sale: 30% markdown
- Required premium = 20% − 8% − (0.15 × 0.30 × 100%) = **7.5%**

Centerbridge targets illiquidity premiums of 8–12% over liquid alternatives. Below that threshold, the risk-adjusted return is insufficient for locking up capital that cannot easily be retrieved.

## Position Sizing in Distressed

### The Illiquidity Premium Framework

Position sizing must account for the inability to exit. In liquid equities, a 10% position in a single stock is aggressive but acceptable. In distressed debt, a 10% position in a single issuer's bonds can be catastrophic if that issuer is the fulcrum in a complex restructuring that takes 4 years to resolve.

**Key principles**:
1. **Size inversely to liquidity**: The less liquid the position, the smaller the allocation (all else equal)
2. **Size inversely to outcome dispersion**: The wider the range of outcomes (fulcrum analysis shows bimodal outcomes — great or zero), the smaller the position
3. **Size proportionally to conviction**: Higher confidence in EV estimate allows larger position
4. **Size inversely to correlation**: Distressed positions with high correlation to each other (same industry, same structure) should be smaller than diversified positions

### The Kelly Criterion Application

The Kelly Criterion (adapted for investing) suggests position sizing as:

Position Size = [Expected Return ÷ Best Case Return × Win Probability] − [Loss ÷ Value at Risk × Loss Probability]

**For distressed specifically**:
- Define "win" = Recovery at or above purchase price
- Define "loss" = Liquidation recovery < 50% of purchase price
- Win probability = probability-weighted sum of base case + bull case
- Loss probability = probability-weighted bear case

**Conservative Kelly application** (distressed investing is too uncertain for full Kelly sizing):
Use fractional Kelly: 0.5 × or 0.25 × the Kelly-recommended size. E.g., if Kelly suggests 12%, size at 3% (0.25 × Kelly).

### Maximum Position Limits

Centerbridge-style distressed portfolios typically enforce:
- **Single-name maximum**: 8–10% of fund NAV in any one issuer's debt (across all tranches)
- **Industry maximum**: 15–20% of fund NAV in any single sector (prevents concentrated cycle exposure)
- **Fulcrum concentration**: Higher limits may apply specifically to fulcrum securities with high conviction, but documented rationale required

## Liquidity Management

### Cash Reserves and Drawdown Facilities

Distressed investing requires maintaining liquidity for two reasons:
1. **Opportunistic buying**: When markets freeze, the best opportunities appear — and only investors with cash can take them
2. **Margin/maintenance requirements**: CDS hedges can require margin posting; distressed bonds might require marking up in a selloff

**Liquidity target**: Maintain 15–20% of the portfolio in highly liquid assets (cash, short-term Treasuries, highly liquid high-yield bonds that can be sold in 48 hours).

**Drawdown facilities**: Maintain committed but undrawn credit facilities (warehouse lines) that can be accessed in 48–72 hours if opportunities exceed cash reserves. The cost of the undrawn facility (typically 25–50 bps per year on committed amount) is insurance against missing opportunities.

### The Exit Timeline Problem

One of the hardest aspects of distressed position sizing: **you cannot know when you will exit.**

Possible exit timelines:
- **Fast**: Pre-packaged bankruptcy with 363 sale — 3–6 months
- **Normal**: Standard Chapter 11 process — 12–24 months
- **Slow**: Litigation, appeals, multi-party disputes — 3–5+ years
- **Never**: Company liquidates; recovery is zero

**Portfolio construction implication**: Build a "liquidity ladder" where:
- 20% of portfolio is expected to exit within 6 months (trading positions, exchange offers)
- 50% of portfolio is expected to exit within 2 years (standard restructurings)
- 30% of portfolio accepts 3–5 year timeline (litigation, complex multi-party restructurings)

This prevents a situation where the entire portfolio is locked up simultaneously and the fund has no ability to meet redemption requests or seize new opportunities.

## Duration Control

### Why Duration Matters in Credit

Duration is a measure of interest rate sensitivity — the percentage change in bond price for a 1% change in interest rates. Longer duration = more rate sensitivity.

In distressed portfolios:
- **High-yield bonds** typically have durations of 3–6 years
- **Leveraged loans** (floating rate) have durations near 0 (rate resets protect from rate moves)
- **Distressed bonds trading at 40–70 cents** have **restructured durations** — their price depends more on credit risk than rate risk, but rising rates still hurt because they depress all bond prices

### Managing Duration in a Distressed Portfolio

| Strategy | When Appropriate | Implementation |
|----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Keep duration short** | Rising rate environment, high inflation | Focus on secured debt, floating-rate bank loans, near-maturity bonds |
| **Hedge with rate swaps** | Long-duration distressed bonds owned | Pay-fixed swaps to neutralize duration exposure |
| **Accept long duration for fulcrum equity** | If the fulcrum converts to equity, duration becomes irrelevant | Size for equity conversion timeline, not bond duration |

**Practical rule**: If the Fed Funds futures market is pricing in rate hikes, reduce portfolio duration by 30–50% relative to normal. Replace long-duration bonds with:
- Floating-rate leveraged loans (duration ~0.5 years)
- Shorter-maturity distressed bonds (maturity <2 years)
- Secured debt that will be repaid in restructuring before long-duration matters

## Concentration Risk vs. Diversification

### The Argument for Concentration in Distressed

In liquid equity investing, diversification reduces unsystematic risk. In distressed investing:
- **Unsystematic risk cannot be diversified away** — each distressed situation has unique legal, operational, and capital structure complexity
- **Research capacity is the limiting factor** — deep due diligence on 20 positions is better than shallow due diligence on 50 positions
- **Returns come from extreme outcomes** — the best distressed investments return 2–5x. Concentration is how you capture that upside.

### The Argument Against Over-Concentration

- **Fulcrum analysis is probabilistic** — even 80% confidence in an EV estimate means 20% chance of being wrong. Over-concentration turns EV estimation errors into catastrophic losses.
- **Liquidity constraints** — positions that are 15%+ of NAV become impossible to exit without market impact
- **Unknown correlations** — distressed positions in different industries may still correlate highly in systematic credit selloffs

### The Centerbridge Balance

**Target concentration range**:
- Top 5 positions: 35–45% of portfolio
- Top 10 positions: 60–70% of portfolio
- Single-name maximum: 10% (except with special circumstances and documented thesis)
- Number of positions: 25–40 (wide enough to diversify unknowns; concentrated enough to generate returns)

**When to concentrate more**:
- Pre-packaged bankruptcies with high conviction and fast timelines
- Rescue financing opportunities where Centerbridge has proprietary information and governance rights
- Fulcrum securities where EV range analysis is tight (e.g., secured by real estate with appraisals)

**When to diversify more**:
- Uncertain macro environments (credit spreads widening, recession risk)
- Litigation-heavy restructurings where timeline and outcome are highly uncertain
- New sectors or structures where Centerbridge has less experience

## The Risk Management Summary

| Risk Type | Primary Tool | Secondary Tool | Cost |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|------|
| **Systematic credit risk** | CDX HY index shorts | Single-name CDS | Carry cost of hedges |
| **Interest rate risk** | Pay-fixed rate swaps | Short Treasury futures | Carry cost + negative convexity |
| **Concentration risk** | Single-name limits (10%) | Industry limits (20%) | Opportunity cost of foregone positions |
| **Liquidity risk** | Cash reserves (15–20%) | Committed drawdown lines | Negative carry on cash; facility fees |
| **Duration risk** | Short-duration bonds | Rate hedges | Lower yield on short-duration |
| **Correlation surprise** | Stress testing | Scenario analysis | Time/effort in modeling |

> "We don't hedge because we're worried about losses. We hedge because we want to be able to buy more when everyone else is selling. Liquidity and dry powder are the most valuable assets in distressed investing. A fully invested portfolio is a portfolio that cannot take advantage of the next CIT."
