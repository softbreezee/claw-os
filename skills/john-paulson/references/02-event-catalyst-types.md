# Event-Driven Catalyst Types

> **When to read this file:** When analyzing non-merger event situations — spin-offs, proxy contests, restructurings, regulatory binary events, capital allocation catalysts. When the opportunity is not a clean M&A spread but rather a corporate event creating a time-bounded return.

The event-driven universe is larger than merger arbitrage. While arb focuses on announced deals, event-driven investing captures returns from ANY corporate event that creates a predictable, time-bound re-pricing of securities. The common thread across all event types: **a specific catalyst that will force price discovery within a defined timeframe**.

> "The event is what separates investing from speculation. Without an identifiable, time-bounded event, you're relying on hope. With it, you're making a probability bet."

---

## The Taxonomy of Event-Driven Catalysts

### Category 1: Spin-offs and Corporate Separations

**What it is**: A parent company distributes shares of a subsidiary to its own shareholders, creating two independent public companies.

**Why Paulson cares**: Spin-offs systematically create mispricings for three reasons:
1. **Forced selling**: Index funds, income funds, and holders of the parent who don't want the spun entity sell immediately, depressing price
2. **Analyst coverage gap**: The spun company has no analyst coverage initially; price discovery is inefficient
3. **Management incentive alignment**: Newly independent management gets equity compensation aligned with the spun entity's stock — motivation to create value that didn't exist when they were inside a conglomerate

**The Paulson Framework for Spin-offs**:

Step 1: **Identify forced sellers** — What type of fund owned the parent? Would they want the spinco?
- If parent is an S&P 500 dividend aristocrat, the spinco (no dividend yet) gets sold by income funds
- If parent is a value fund holding, the spinco (often growth, capex-intensive) gets sold by value funds
- If parent is a large-cap holding, the spinco (often small-cap) gets sold by large-cap-only funds

Step 2: **Analyze spinco standalone quality** — Without the parent, is this a good business?
- Does it have its own competitive advantage?
- Was it held back inside the parent (capital allocation, brand, distribution)?
- Is management strong and newly incentivized?

Step 3: **Wait for selling pressure to exhaust** — Typically 30-90 days post-distribution

Step 4: **Establish position** during the forced selling window, then hold for 12-18 months as natural holders accumulate and analyst coverage initiates

**Historical return data**: Spin-offs have historically outperformed the market by 10-15% in the first year (Penn study; Greenblatt's documented cases)

**Key risk**: The spinco genuinely is inferior — it was separated because the parent didn't want it, not because separation unlocked value.

### Category 2: Proxy Contests and Activist Catalysts

**What it is**: A dissident shareholder campaigns to replace board members (proxy contest) or drive specific corporate changes (activist campaign) without necessarily seeking board control.

**Why Paulson cares**: Activist campaigns create a time-bounded catalyst for value realization. The proxy contest has a fixed deadline (annual meeting date), creating a specific event horizon.

**Subtype A: Proxy Contest (Board Control)**
- Activist nominates competing slate of directors
- Annual meeting date is the catalyst (typically within 6-12 months)
- Success probability anchored to: activist's % ownership, ISS/Glass Lewis recommendation, major institutional holder positions
- **Paulson positions**: Buy target shares early in the campaign; analyze probability of activist success and value of proposed changes

**Subtype B: Activist Campaign (Strategic)**
- Activist demands spin-off, buyback, CEO change, sale process, or other strategic action
- Catalyst timeline: negotiated settlement or escalation to proxy contest
- Value realization: often via sale, spin-off, or capital return — links back to other catalyst types

**Framework for Probability Assessment**:
```
P(activist succeeds) = f(
  Activist ownership %,        → higher % = more leverage
  ISS/GL recommendation,       → institutional proxy services have outsized influence
  Track record of activist,    → known activists get more deference
  Target management quality,   → weak management = easier target
  Institutional support,       → % of shares in index funds vs. engaged institutions
  Specific demands' validity   → are the demands financially reasonable?
)
```

**Typical return profile**: 
- Partial success (some demands met): +10-20% over 12-18 months
- Full success (sale): +30-50%+ depending on premium
- Failure: -5 to -15% as arb community exits

### Category 3: Corporate Restructurings

**What it is**: A company reorganizes its balance sheet or business structure — debt exchange, equity raise, asset sale, merger of equals, or bankruptcy.

**Subcategory A: Out-of-Court Restructuring**
- Company negotiates with creditors outside bankruptcy
- Exchange offer: offer creditors new securities (less face value) to reduce debt load
- **Catalyst**: Consummation of exchange offer or debt-for-equity swap
- **Paulson view**: Buy distressed debt if the exchange terms are favorable; or short the equity if the restructuring will dilute significantly

**Subcategory B: Divisional Divestitures**
- Company sells a non-core division, using proceeds for debt reduction, buyback, or reinvestment
- **Catalyst**: Completion of divestiture and announcement of use of proceeds
- **Paulson play**: If company is trading at a discount to sum-of-parts, buy; the divestiture crystallizes the hidden value

**Subcategory C: Leveraged Recapitalizations**
- Company takes on debt to pay a special dividend, triggering re-rating
- **Catalyst**: Shareholder approval of recap; special dividend payment date
- **Return driver**: Special dividend provides immediate cash return; stock re-rates based on new capital structure

### Category 4: Regulatory Binary Events

**What it is**: A regulatory decision will either approve or deny a company's ability to do something — drug approval, spectrum auction win/loss, tariff ruling, license grant.

**Why Paulson cares**: Regulatory binary events have defined timelines (FDA PDUFA dates, auction schedules) and create asymmetric option-like return profiles.

**The FDA Binary Example**:
- Drug with $3B peak sales potential awaiting FDA approval decision on PDUFA date (X months away)
- Stock price reflects ~60% market-implied P(approval)
- If Paulson believes P(approval) = 80% (higher than market), the expected-value calculation is favorable

**Framework for Regulatory Probability Assessment**:
1. **Historical base rate**: FDA approves ~85-90% of drugs at PDUFA for NDA/BLA with complete response
2. **Advisory committee vote**: adcom recommendation is highly predictive (~90% of FDA decisions follow adcom)
3. **CRL history**: Has this drug had a Complete Response Letter before? Second cycles have lower approval rates
4. **Competitive context**: Is there an urgent unmet medical need? Expedited pathway (Breakthrough Therapy)?
5. **Manufacturing risk**: CMC issues often cause delays even for efficacious drugs

**Positioning approach**: 
- Pure equity: High upside if approved, significant downside if rejected
- Options: Buy calls before PDUFA date; the options delta captures the probability asymmetry
- Hedged: Long stock + protective puts (expensive due to high IV, but defined risk)

> "FDA binary events are not merger arb — they're more like options pricing. You're making a bet that the market's implied probability is wrong. You need a genuine informational or analytical edge."

### Category 5: Capital Allocation Events

**What it is**: Management announces a specific capital return program — buyback, special dividend, rights offering — that creates a time-bounded value crystallization.

**Subcategory A: Share Buyback Authorizations**
- Company announces $Xbn buyback at/below current prices
- **Catalyst**: Execution of buyback reduces share count, mechanically increases EPS
- **Paulson interest**: Only when buyback is large (>5% of market cap) AND shares trade below intrinsic value AND management has commitment to execute
- **Risk**: Announced buybacks are not always executed; companies routinely announce and then de-prioritize

**Subcategory B: Special Dividends**
- Triggered by asset sale, litigation settlement, or balance sheet optimization
- **Catalyst**: Record date for special dividend
- **Paulson play**: Buy before record date; dividend provides immediate return; analyze whether ex-dividend price drop fully discounts the payment

**Subcategory C: Rights Offerings**
- Company offers existing shareholders right to buy new shares at a discount
- **Catalyst**: Rights exercise/expiration date
- **Arbitrage**: Rights have a calculable fair value; if trading below fair value (common early in offering), buy rights; hedge by shorting shares

## Catalyst Timing and Probability Matrix

| Catalyst Type | Typical Timeline | Probability Range | Key Driver of Probability |
|--------------|----------------|------------------|--------------------------|
| Cash merger close | 4-12 months | 75-95% | Regulatory + financing |
| Stock merger close | 4-12 months | 70-90% | Shareholder vote + regulatory |
| Spin-off (forced seller opportunity) | 1-3 months post-distribution | 85-95% (will happen) | Value depends on spinco quality |
| Proxy contest (annual meeting) | 3-9 months | 40-70% | Activist leverage + ISS |
| FDA PDUFA decision | Known date | 60-90% | Clinical data + adcom |
| Out-of-court restructuring | 3-12 months | 50-75% | Creditor coordination |
| Special dividend payment | 1-3 months | 90-98% | Board authorization done |
| Divestiture completion | 3-9 months | 75-90% | Regulatory + buyer financing |

## The Catalyst Quality Assessment Framework

Not all catalysts are equal. Score each catalyst on three dimensions:

**1. Specificity** (1-5): Is the catalyst precisely defined, or vague?
- 5 = "FDA PDUFA date: March 15, 2025 — drug approval/rejection"
- 1 = "Management says they'll 'explore strategic alternatives'"

**2. Controllability** (1-5): Can the catalyst be derailed by outside forces?
- 5 = Special dividend — board vote already approved, record date set
- 1 = Proxy contest — depends on other shareholders' votes, ISS opinion, settlement negotiations

**3. Time-Boundedness** (1-5): Is there a hard deadline?
- 5 = Rights offering expiration date — rights expire and become worthless
- 1 = "Share buyback at management's discretion over the next 3 years"

**Minimum score to proceed**: Total ≥ 9 out of 15

> "The perfect catalyst is specific, largely within the control of one or two parties, and has a hard deadline. The worst 'catalyst' is management saying they're 'committed to shareholder value.'"

## No Catalyst = No Trade: The Iron Rule

Paulson's absolute discipline: **every position must have an identifiable, time-bound catalyst**. Without it:

- There is no forcing function for price realization
- Holding period becomes indefinite (capital tied up with no return horizon)
- Returns depend on unpredictable sentiment changes rather than analyzable events
- Position competes with other event-driven opportunities with real catalysts

> "If someone pitches me an idea and I ask 'What's the catalyst?' and they say 'Management is going to eventually realize the value,' I stop listening. That's not a catalyst. That's a hope."

**The test**: "What specific event, occurring on or before date X, will cause the security to re-price to fair value?" If you cannot answer this with specificity, the trade does not belong in an event-driven portfolio.
