# Deal Precedents Database

> **When to read this file:** When pattern-matching a current deal to historical precedents, when estimating deal completion probability based on comparable transactions, when assessing how regulators have handled similar sector consolidations, or when studying what separates deals that close from deals that break.

Pattern-matching to historical precedents is core to Paulson's merger arb discipline. No deal is unique — every deal shares characteristics with prior deals, and those precedents provide the most reliable empirical anchor for probability estimation. The analyst who knows 50 historical deals thoroughly can price risk far better than one relying on abstract models.

> "I can look at a deal and within 10 minutes know what the three most similar historical deals were. That database — built over 30 years — is one of our most valuable assets."

## Case Study 1: AT&T / Time Warner (2016-2018)

**Deal**: AT&T acquires Time Warner for $85.4bn ($107.50/share), announced October 2016
**Type**: Vertical merger (telecom + content); cash deal
**Regulatory**: DOJ Antitrust Division reviewed; no foreign jurisdiction issues

**Deal Dimensions Scorecard at Announcement**:
- Antitrust: Yellow → Red (vertical deal, but DOJ challenged it — unusual for vertical mergers)
- Financing: Green (AT&T's committed debt financing; no MAC allowing exit)
- Shareholder Vote: Green (Time Warner shareholders approved overwhelmingly)
- Closing Conditions: Yellow (DOJ challenge created uncertainty)
- Break Fee: Green ($500M reverse break fee; "hell-or-high-water" clause)
- Strategic Rationale: Green (content + distribution; plausible industrial logic)
- Interloper: Green (no competing bidder materialized)

**Timeline**:
- Announcement: October 2016
- DOJ files to block: November 2017 (unusual — DOJ had not challenged a vertical merger in 40 years)
- District Court rules for AT&T: June 2018 (deal closes)
- DOJ appeal rejected: February 2019

**Spread behavior**:
- At announcement: spread ~3-4%
- After DOJ lawsuit (Nov 2017): spread widened to ~8-10%
- After district court win (June 2018): spread collapsed; deal closed

**Outcome**: Deal closed. DOJ lost.

**Lessons**:
1. Vertical mergers historically face much lower regulatory risk than horizontal — but the Trump DOJ broke precedent
2. "Hell-or-high-water" clauses matter: AT&T could NOT walk away from the deal; it had to fight the DOJ
3. The spread widening after the DOJ lawsuit created a buying opportunity for those who correctly assessed the legal risk
4. Judicial precedent on vertical mergers was well-established in AT&T's favor — legal analysis, not just regulatory analysis, mattered

**Applicable to**: Any vertical merger where DOJ or FTC may challenge despite weak legal precedent for intervention

## Case Study 2: Microsoft / Activision Blizzard (2022-2023)

**Deal**: Microsoft acquires Activision Blizzard for $68.7bn ($95/share), announced January 2022
**Type**: Horizontal + vertical (gaming publisher + gaming platform); cash deal
**Regulatory**: FTC (US), CMA (UK), EU, multiple foreign jurisdictions

**Deal Dimensions Scorecard at Announcement**:
- Antitrust: Red (FTC hostile; CMA took unprecedented stance; EU reviewed)
- Financing: Green (Microsoft had $100bn+ cash; no financing needed)
- Shareholder Vote: Green (ATVI shareholders approved)
- Closing Conditions: Red (required regulatory clearance in 40+ jurisdictions)
- Break Fee: Green ($3B reverse break fee; "hell-or-high-water" commitments)
- Strategic Rationale: Green (gaming metaverse; Call of Duty as key IP)
- Interloper: Green (no competing bid)

**Timeline**:
- Announcement: January 2022
- FTC sues to block: December 2022
- CMA provisionally blocks: April 2023 (shocking; UK = most difficult hurdle)
- EU clears deal: May 2023
- FTC injunction denied by federal judge: July 2023
- CMA reverses provisional decision and clears deal: July 2023
- Deal closes: October 2023 (extended from original April 2023 target)

**Spread behavior**:
- At announcement: ~4%
- Peak spread (after CMA provisional block): ~20-25% ($75-78 vs. $95 deal price)
- Post-CMA/FTC resolution: collapsed to ~1%

**Outcome**: Deal closed. All regulators cleared or were overruled.

**Lessons**:
1. Multi-jurisdiction review = timeline risk even for deals that ultimately close
2. CMA has become one of the most aggressive global regulators — UK deals require extra scrutiny
3. At $75-78 with a $95 cash deal price and "hell-or-high-water," the risk/reward was excellent for those who could hold through the CMA uncertainty
4. Microsoft's enormous balance sheet meant financing was never a risk — allowed management to fight, not settle
5. FTC under Khan took unprecedented positions; courts ultimately sided against the FTC on injunctive relief
6. $3B reverse break fee ($95 × ~3.5%) provided a clear downside anchor: if deal broke, ATVI would receive $3B + trade as a standalone

**Applicable to**: Technology M&A facing hostile FTC/CMA; deals with foreign jurisdiction complexity

## Case Study 3: Sprint / T-Mobile (2018-2020)

**Deal**: T-Mobile acquires Sprint for implied value ~$26bn (0.10256 T-Mobile shares per Sprint share), announced April 2018
**Type**: Horizontal (4→3 wireless carriers); stock deal
**Regulatory**: DOJ, FCC; state AG litigation

**Deal Dimensions Scorecard at Announcement**:
- Antitrust: Red (4→3 in mobile wireless; prior AT&T/T-Mobile blocked 2011)
- Financing: Green (equity funded by Deutsche Telekom/SoftBank; no debt financing needed)
- Shareholder Vote: Green (approved)
- Closing Conditions: Red (DOJ approval critical; FCC approval needed; 14 state AGs challenged)
- Break Fee: Yellow ($600M reverse break fee; $600M break fee; "hell-or-high-water")
- Strategic Rationale: Yellow (5G buildout argument; Sprint's network was struggling)
- Interloper: Green (no third bidder)

**Timeline**:
- Announcement: April 2018
- DOJ clearance (with DISH divestiture): July 2019
- FCC clearance: November 2019
- State AG lawsuit rejected: February 2020
- Deal closes: April 2020

**Spread behavior**:
- At announcement: ~10-15% (high due to antitrust risk perception from AT&T/T-Mobile precedent)
- After DOJ approval: spread collapsed
- Deal took 2 years to close — significant carry on the spread

**Outcome**: Deal closed. DOJ approved with DISH remedy.

**Lessons**:
1. Prior blocking of AT&T/T-Mobile (2011) was strong negative precedent — justified initial wide spread
2. The DISH remedy (divestiture to create new 4th carrier) was the key to DOJ approval — remedies matter
3. Stock deals have acquirer share price risk — Sprint holders' return depended on T-Mobile's stock price
4. Timeline extension to 2 years reduced annualized return despite ultimate success
5. State AG litigation created second wave of risk post-DOJ clearance — foreign/state AG litigation now a real risk in large telecom deals
6. "Precedent can be overcome with the right remedy" — critical insight for antitrust analysis

**Applicable to**: Horizontal telecom/cable deals; situations where prior blocking seems to create strong negative precedent

## Case Study 4: Pfizer / Allergan (2015-2016) — The Break

**Deal**: Pfizer acquires Allergan for $160bn (largest pharma deal ever), announced November 2015
**Type**: Inversion deal (Pfizer relocating domicile to Ireland); cash-stock deal
**Regulatory**: IRS regulatory risk (not competition law)

**Deal Dimensions Scorecard**:
- Antitrust: Green (minimal product overlap; pharma with different therapeutic areas)
- Financing: Yellow/Green (mostly stock deal; Pfizer could fund)
- Shareholder Vote: Green (approved)
- Closing Conditions: Red (explicit regulatory risk of IRS action against inversions)
- Break Fee: Yellow (deal had unusual regulatory out for inversion rule changes)
- Strategic Rationale: Green (tax savings were the primary driver — $21bn over 5 years)
- Interloper: Green

**Timeline**:
- Announcement: November 2015
- Obama Treasury issues anti-inversion rules: April 2016
- Deal terminated: April 2016 (deal terms made inversion benefit disappear)

**Spread behavior**:
- At announcement: ~5-7%
- Day deal breaks: Allergan stock falls ~30%; Pfizer stock rises

**Outcome**: Deal BROKE. Allergan shareholders suffered significant losses.

**Lessons**:
1. Regulatory risk is not only competition law — tax/regulatory change risk exists for inversion deals, infrastructure deals, etc.
2. The "regulatory out" in the merger agreement — allowing Pfizer to walk if inversion laws changed — was deal-breaking language that arbs should have weighted heavily
3. Political risk (Obama administration vocal opposition to inversions) was a real, material risk
4. Deals driven primarily by financial engineering (tax, not strategy) are more fragile than strategically-driven deals — the buyer has less motivation to fight
5. Downside at break (-30%+) validated the importance of break risk analysis
6. Allergan had significant standalone value, so break wasn't catastrophic — but still painful

**Applicable to**: Any deal where the strategic rationale is primarily financial engineering; deals with explicit regulatory/political risk beyond competition law

## Case Study 5: Cigna / Express Scripts (2018)

**Deal**: Cigna acquires Express Scripts for $67bn ($48.75 cash + 0.2434 Cigna shares), announced March 2018
**Type**: Vertical (health insurer + PBM); cash + stock deal
**Regulatory**: DOJ reviewed; FTC had prior interest in PBM concentration

**Deal Dimensions Scorecard**:
- Antitrust: Yellow (vertical deal; DOJ cleared AT&T/TW going through litigation simultaneously)
- Financing: Yellow (large cash component; Cigna needed significant debt financing)
- Shareholder Vote: Yellow (Cigna shareholders initially resistant — dilutive acquisition)
- Closing Conditions: Yellow (DoD/VA insurance contracts at risk)
- Break Fee: Green (large reverse break fee; commitment to close)
- Strategic Rationale: Green (vertical integration; managed care + PBM trend)
- Interloper: Green

**Timeline**:
- Announcement: March 2018
- Cigna shareholder vote approved: August 2018
- DOJ clearance: September 2018
- Deal closes: December 2018

**Spread behavior**:
- At announcement: ~10% (wide for ultimately smooth deal due to Cigna shareholder risk)
- After shareholder vote approval: narrowed significantly

**Outcome**: Deal closed. Spread was attractive for the risk.

**Lessons**:
1. Acquirer shareholder opposition to a deal creates a real closing risk — "shareholder vote" dimension includes acquirer vote, not just target
2. Vertical deals in healthcare face unique regulatory scrutiny — but ultimately DOJ cleared this one similarly to AT&T/TW
3. Healthcare sector deals often have government contract risks (DoD, VA, CMS) that pure competition analysis misses
4. Wide initial spread (10%) reflected multiple risk dimensions — diversified risk, not just antitrust

## Case Study 6: Qualcomm / NXP Semiconductors (2016-2018) — The Break

**Deal**: Qualcomm acquires NXP for $39bn ($110/share raised to $127.50), announced 2016
**Type**: Horizontal (semiconductors); cash deal
**Regulatory**: Required approval in 9 jurisdictions including China (MOFCOM)

**Outcome**: Deal BROKE in July 2018 when China (MOFCOM) did not grant approval by deadline during US-China trade war

**Lessons**:
1. China MOFCOM approval is now a veto right on global semiconductor deals — cannot be modeled as "mechanical approval"
2. Geopolitical risk is a real dimension in M&A — especially in tech/semiconductors where China has leverage
3. Deal breaks in stock cash deals require separate analysis — NXP traded at ~$88 post-break vs. $127.50 deal price (31% gap)
4. Trade war timing created systemic risk that was hard to model from fundamentals

## Pattern Recognition Framework

When analyzing a new deal, find the best historical comparable by scoring:

| Characteristic | Deal A (current) | Best Comparable |
|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Sector | [tech/pharma/telecom/etc.] | Match sector |
| Deal type | [horizontal/vertical] | Match type |
| Size (deal value) | [$Xbn] | ±50% range |
| Cash vs. stock | [cash/stock/mixed] | Match |
| Primary regulator | [DOJ/FTC/CFIUS/China] | Match |
| Strategic rationale | [growth/synergies/financial] | Match |
| Break fee structure | [% of deal value] | Match |
| Acquirer strength | [strategic/PE/cross-border] | Match |

**Completion rates by category** (historical base rates):
- Strategic horizontal, DOJ cleared by remedy: ~75-80%
- Strategic horizontal, no major antitrust: ~90-92%
- Strategic vertical: ~80-85% (post AT&T/TW era)
- PE/LBO with committed financing: ~78-83%
- Cross-border with CFIUS: ~70-80%
- Cross-border with China MOFCOM required: ~60-75% (declining trend)
- Inversion deals (tax-motivated): ~65-75% (high regulatory/political risk)

> "The database is never perfect — every deal has unique characteristics. But starting with the right base rate from the closest comparable prevents the most common error: assuming this deal will complete just because 'it makes strategic sense.'"
