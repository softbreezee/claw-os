# Special Situations Beyond M&A: Opportunistic Event-Driven Strategies

> **When to read this file:** When identifying event-driven investment opportunities outside traditional merger arbitrage, when evaluating post-bankruptcy equities for stigma-driven mispricing, when analyzing SPAC arbitrage opportunities pre-deal, when assessing rights offerings and tender offers for premium capture, when evaluating stub trades from spin-offs, or when considering activism-adjacent positions that benefit from activist catalysts without running campaigns.

Merger arbitrage is the core of event-driven investing, but it is not the only strategy. Paulson's firm has generated significant returns from a diverse set of special situations — corporate events that create predictable price dislocations due to structural forced selling, investor inertia, regulatory complexity, or psychological biases. These situations share common features: a defined catalyst, a measurable gap between current price and intrinsic value, and a reason why the market has not yet arbitraged the gap away. Understanding these strategies expands the opportunity set beyond traditional merger arb.

> "Merger arb is about capturing spreads on announced deals. Special situations are about identifying events that will create value — and investing before the market fully recognizes the opportunity. The common thread is catalysts. Without a catalyst, you are just a value investor hoping for recognition. With a catalyst, you are an event-driven investor with a timeline." — Paulson & Co.

> "The best special situations are those where the market is forced to sell for reasons unrelated to value. Bankruptcy stigma, index exclusion, forced selling from rights offerings — these create dislocations that we can exploit. We are not betting on business outcomes. We are betting on the mechanics of corporate events." — Paulson & Co.

## Strategy 1: Post-Bankruptcy Equities

### The Stigma Dislocation

When a company emerges from Chapter 11 bankruptcy, its equity is often severely undervalued due to investor stigma and structural forced selling.

**Why post-bankruptcy equities are mispriced:**

1. **Institutional mandate restrictions:**
   - Many institutional investors (pension funds, endowments, mutual funds) are prohibited from holding stocks of companies that have been in bankruptcy
   - These investors must sell regardless of valuation
   - The selling pressure creates a discount to intrinsic value

2. **Index exclusion:**
   - Post-bankruptcy equities are typically excluded from major indices initially
   - Index funds and ETFs do not hold the stock
   - Passive buying pressure is absent

3. **Analyst coverage gap:**
   - Sell-side analysts often drop coverage during bankruptcy
   - Post-emergence, coverage is sparse or non-existent
   - Information asymmetry creates uncertainty discount

4. **Psychological stigma:**
   - Retail investors associate "bankruptcy" with "failure"
   - The stock is perceived as risky regardless of the restructured balance sheet
   - This perception creates a persistent discount

**The Paulson framework for post-bankruptcy investing:**

```
Step 1: Analyze the reorganization plan
  - What is the new capital structure?
  - How much debt was eliminated?
  - What is the pro forma equity value based on comparable company multiples?

Step 2: Assess the stigma discount
  - Compare post-emergence market cap to pro forma intrinsic value
  - Typical discount: 30-50% initially
  - Catalyst for closure: Index inclusion, analyst coverage initiation, institutional buying

Step 3: Evaluate the business fundamentals
  - Is the core business viable post-restructuring?
  - Has the company shed unprofitable divisions?
  - Is management credible (new team or reformed existing team)?

Step 4: Identify the catalyst timeline
  - Index inclusion typically occurs 6-18 months post-emergence
  - Analyst coverage initiates within 3-6 months
  - Institutional buying begins once stigma fades (12-24 months)

Entry: Buy immediately post-emergence or within first 3 months
Exit: When discount narrows to <15% or after 18-24 months if catalysts have played out
```

### Historical Examples

**General Motors (2009-2010):**
- Bankruptcy: June 2009 (government-backed restructuring)
- Post-emergence equity: "New GM" IPO November 2010
- Initial valuation: ~$35B market cap
- Intrinsic value: Paulson and others estimated $50-60B based on turnaround plan
- Outcome: Stock appreciated 50%+ in first 12 months post-IPO as stigma faded

**American Airlines (2013-2015):**
- Bankruptcy: November 2011
- Emergence: December 2013 (merged with US Airways)
- Post-emergence equity: Significant stigma discount initially
- Catalyst: Index inclusion, renewed analyst coverage, industry consolidation narrative
- Outcome: Stock appreciated 200%+ over 3 years post-emergence

### Post-Bankruptcy Checklist

- [ ] **Reorganization plan confirmed:** Court has approved the plan; emergence timeline is certain
- [ ] **Balance sheet analysis:** Pro forma debt/EBITDA < 3x; manageable interest expense
- [ ] **Stigma assessment:** Major institutional holders have exited; float is dominated by distressed funds and retail
- [ ] **Catalyst timeline:** Index inclusion expected within 12-18 months; analyst coverage initiating
- [ ] **Valuation gap:** Market cap is >30% below comparable company multiples
- [ ] **Management quality:** New CEO or credible turnaround plan from existing management
- [ ] **Industry dynamics:** Sector is not in structural decline (avoid post-bankruptcy companies in dying industries)

## Strategy 2: SPAC Arbitrage (Pre-Deal)

### The NAV Floor

SPACs (Special Purpose Acquisition Companies) raise capital through an IPO with the mandate to acquire a private company within 18-24 months. Before a deal is announced, SPAC shares typically trade near their NAV (Net Asset Value) — the cash held in trust.

**The arbitrage mechanics:**

```
SPAC IPO structure:
  - IPO price: $10.00 per share
  - Cash in trust: ~$9.80-$10.00 per share (after underwriting fees)
  - Warrants: Included as "sweetener" (typically 1/3 or 1/2 warrant per share)
  - Warrant terms: Exercise price $11.50, exercisable only after business combination

Pre-deal trading dynamics:
  - SPAC shares trade at $9.80-$10.20 typically
  - Downside: Limited to NAV floor ($9.80-$10.00) — shareholders can redeem at NAV
  - Upside: If a good deal is announced, shares can rally to $12-$15+
  - Warrant value: Additional optionality on the combined company

The arbitrage:
  - Buy SPAC at or below NAV (e.g., $9.80)
  - Downside: Redeem at $10.00 if no deal or bad deal (risk-free or near risk-free)
  - Upside: If a credible deal is announced, shares appreciate
  - Expected return: 5-15% annualized with limited downside
```

**The Paulson SPAC screen:**

| Criterion | Ideal | Acceptable | Avoid |
|-----------|-------|------------|-------|
| Trust value per share | $10.00 | $9.80-$9.95 | <$9.80 |
| Current share price | ≤$9.80 | $9.80-$10.00 | >$10.10 |
| Time to deadline | >12 months | 6-12 months | <6 months |
| Sponsor quality | Top-tier PE/VC firm | Experienced operator | First-time sponsor |
| Target sector | High-growth, credible | Established industry | Speculative/undefined |
| Warrant terms | 1/2 warrant, $11.50 strike | 1/3 warrant, $11.50 strike | No warrants or unfavorable terms |

**Risks to the arbitrage:**

1. **Liquidation risk:** If no deal is completed by the deadline, the SPAC liquidates and returns NAV. This is not a loss, but it eliminates the upside and locks up capital for 18-24 months at 0% return.

2. **Bad deal risk:** If a value-destructive deal is announced, shareholders can vote against it or redeem. However, some SPAC structures have low redemption thresholds that allow bad deals to close.

3. **Opportunity cost:** Capital is tied up for 18-24 months with uncertain upside. In a strong market, this opportunity cost can be significant.

4. **Dilution risk:** Sponsor promote (typically 20% of equity) dilutes public shareholders.

### SPAC Arbitrage Execution

```
Step 1: Screen for SPACs trading at or below NAV
  - Use SPAC tracking databases (SPAC Research, SPAC Insider)
  - Filter: Price ≤ NAV, >12 months to deadline, reputable sponsor

Step 2: Analyze the sponsor
  - Track record of previous SPACs or PE investments
  - Industry expertise aligned with stated target sector
  - Skin in the game (sponsor has invested personal capital)

Step 3: Evaluate the trust structure
  - Confirm NAV per share in trust account
  - Review redemption rights (can shareholders redeem at NAV?)
  - Check for any unusual terms (high sponsor promote, low redemption threshold)

Step 4: Build position
  - Accumulate at or below NAV
  - Size: 1-3% per SPAC (diversify across 10-20 SPACs)
  - Hold until deal announcement or liquidation

Step 5: Exit decision
  - If deal announced: Evaluate deal quality
    - Good deal: Hold or add for upside
    - Bad deal: Redeem at NAV or sell pre-vote
  - If no deal by deadline: Redeem at NAV (return of capital)
```

## Strategy 3: Rights Offerings

### The Forced Selling Opportunity

A rights offering gives existing shareholders the right to purchase additional shares at a discount to market price. Rights offerings often create temporary mispricing due to forced selling by shareholders who do not wish to participate.

**The mechanics:**

```
Rights offering structure:
  - Company announces rights offering: "Shareholders receive 1 right per share"
  - Rights allow purchase of new shares at discount (e.g., 15-25% below market)
  - Rights are tradable (can be sold in the market if shareholder doesn't want to participate)
  - Subscription period: Typically 2-4 weeks

The dislocation:
  - Some shareholders do not want to participate (don't want to invest more, or cannot due to mandate)
  - These shareholders sell their rights in the market
  - Forced selling pressure drives rights price below theoretical value
  - Arbitrageurs buy rights and subscribe for discounted shares
  - Profit = Discount on shares - cost of rights

Example:
  - Stock price: $50
  - Rights offering: 1 right + $40 = 1 new share (20% discount)
  - Theoretical rights value: $10 (difference between market price and subscription price)
  - Market price of rights (due to forced selling): $8
  - Arbitrage: Buy rights at $8, pay $40, receive share worth $50
  - Profit: $50 - $40 - $8 = $2 per share (4% return over 2-4 weeks = 50-100% annualized)
```

**The Paulson rights offering framework:**

| Factor | Favorable | Unfavorable |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| Discount to market price | >20% | <15% |
| Rights trading price | Below theoretical value | At or above theoretical value |
| Subscription period | 3-4 weeks (more time to trade) | 1-2 weeks (less time to arbitrage) |
| Underwriter backstop | Yes (reduces risk of under-subscription) | No (risk of deal not completing) |
| Company fundamentals | Stable or improving | Deteriorating (stock may fall during subscription period) |
| Float liquidity | High (easy to enter/exit) | Low (illiquid rights market) |

### Rights Offering Execution

```
Step 1: Identify rights offerings
  - Monitor 8-K filings for rights offering announcements
  - Use data providers (Bloomberg, FactSet) to screen for rights offerings

Step 2: Calculate theoretical rights value
  - Formula: Rights Value = (Market Price - Subscription Price) / (N + 1)
    Where N = number of rights needed to buy one share
  - Compare to market price of rights

Step 3: Assess the discount and timeline
  - Discount >20%: Attractive
  - Subscription period >2 weeks: Sufficient time to execute
  - Backstop commitment: Reduces completion risk

Step 4: Execute the arbitrage
  - Buy rights in the market (if trading below theoretical value)
  - Subscribe for new shares by paying subscription price
  - Sell shares immediately upon receipt (or hold if company is fundamentally attractive)

Step 5: Risk management
  - Stock price risk: If stock falls during subscription period, arbitrage profit shrinks
  - Hedge: Short the underlying stock (if rights are sufficiently cheap to cover short cost)
  - Completion risk: Ensure underwriter backstop is in place
```

## Strategy 4: Tender Offers

### Premium Capture

A tender offer is a public bid to purchase shares from existing shareholders at a specified price (usually at a premium to market). Tender offers create opportunities to capture the premium with defined risk.

**The tender offer arbitrage:**

```
Structure:
  - Acquirer (or company itself) announces tender offer: "We will buy up to X million shares at $Y per share"
  - Offer price is typically 15-30% above current market price
  - Offer period: 20-60 days (regulated by SEC Rule 14e)
  - Conditions: May be subject to minimum number of shares tendered, regulatory approval, etc.

The arbitrage:
  - Buy shares after announcement at price between current market and offer price
  - Tender shares into the offer
  - Receive offer price when tender closes
  - Profit = Offer Price - Purchase Price - Transaction Costs

Example:
  - Pre-announcement price: $40
  - Tender offer price: $50 (25% premium)
  - Post-announcement price: $48 (market prices in some deal risk)
  - Arbitrage: Buy at $48, tender at $50
  - Profit: $2 per share (4.2% over 30-60 days = 25-50% annualized)
```

**Key risks:**

1. **Deal risk:** The tender offer may not complete (conditions not met, acquirer walks away)
2. **Proration risk:** If more shares are tendered than the acquirer wants to buy, shareholders receive only a portion of their shares at the offer price
3. **Timing risk:** If the offer period is extended, annualized return decreases

### Tender Offer Checklist

- [ ] **Offer price:** Premium >20% to pre-announcement price
- [ ] **Conditions:** Minimal conditions (avoid offers subject to financing, regulatory, or material adverse change clauses)
- [ ] **Backstop:** Acquirer has demonstrated financial capacity to complete
- [ ] **Proration:** Offer is for "all or none" or proration risk is acceptable
- [ ] **Timeline:** Offer period <60 days (longer periods reduce annualized return)
- [ ] **Regulatory:** No significant antitrust or regulatory hurdles expected

## Strategy 5: Stub Trades (Spin-Off Mispricing)

### The Parent/Child Dislocation

When a company spins off a subsidiary, the parent company often retains a "stub" value — the market cap of the parent excluding the value of the spun-off entity. Stub trades exploit situations where the sum-of-parts valuation is significantly higher than the combined market cap.

**The stub trade mechanics:**

```
Spin-off structure:
  - Parent Company A owns 100% of Subsidiary B
  - Parent announces spin-off: "Shareholders will receive 1 share of B for each share of A"
  - After spin-off: A and B trade independently

The mispricing:
  - Pre-spin: Market cap of A = $10B (includes 100% of B)
  - Estimated value of B: $4B (based on comparables)
  - Implied stub value of A (ex-B): $6B
  - Post-spin: A trades at $5B, B trades at $3B
  - Sum-of-parts: $8B vs. pre-spin implied $10B
  - Dislocation: $2B discount (20%)

Why the discount exists:
  - Index funds must sell one of the entities (if it doesn't fit their index criteria)
  - Forced selling creates temporary pressure
  - Analyst coverage lags (B may not be covered initially)
  - Investor confusion (A is now a different business; investors need time to reassess)

The trade:
  - Long the stub (Parent A post-spin)
  - Short comparables (to hedge out industry beta)
  - Wait for sum-of-parts recognition (6-18 months typical)
```

**Historical examples:**

**eBay / PayPal (2015):**
- eBay spun off PayPal in July 2015
- Post-spin: Both entities were initially undervalued as standalone companies
- PayPal: Became one of the best-performing fintech stocks (5x return over 5 years)
- eBay: Stub value recognized as marketplace business was re-rated

**Dell / VMware (2021):**
- Dell held an 81% stake in VMware
- Dell's market cap often traded below the value of its VMware stake alone
- Stub trade: Long Dell, short VMware (to isolate the value of Dell's core business)
- Catalyst: Dell eventually spun off VMware, unlocking value

### Stub Trade Checklist

- [ ] **Sum-of-parts discount:** Combined value of parent + subsidiary >20% above combined market cap
- [ ] **Catalyst:** Spin-off completed or announced with clear timeline
- [ ] **Forced selling:** Index funds or institutions are likely to sell one entity (creates entry opportunity)
- [ ] **Coverage gap:** Subsidiary will initially have limited analyst coverage (creates information asymmetry)
- [ ] **Business quality:** Both entities are fundamentally sound (avoid stubs where one business is deteriorating)
- [ ] **Liquidity:** Both stocks are liquid enough to establish and exit positions

## Strategy 6: Activism-Adjacent Positions

### Riding Alongside Activists

Running an activist campaign is resource-intensive and risky. An alternative is to identify companies that are likely activist targets and invest alongside the anticipated activist — benefiting from the catalyst without the cost and risk of leading the campaign.

**The activism-adjacent framework:**

```
Step 1: Screen for activist targets
  - Governance failures (see Paulson 06-governance-analysis.md)
  - Sustained TSR underperformance vs. peers (3-5 years)
  - Valuation discount to intrinsic value (30%+)
  - Concentrated insider ownership (<40%) — leaves enough float for activist to accumulate
  - Recent governance changes (new board members, CEO transition) — signals openness to change

Step 2: Monitor 13D filings
  - When an activist files a 13D (crosses 5% ownership), the campaign is public
  - Entry: Buy within days of 13D filing (before the broader market fully prices in the activist premium)
  - Rationale: Activist campaigns typically create 10-30% price appreciation over 6-18 months

Step 3: Assess the activist's track record
  - Elliott, Starboard, ValueAct, Third Point, Icahn: High success rate
  - First-time activists or unknown funds: Higher risk, higher potential reward
  - Match the activist's style to the situation (governance-focused, operational, M&A-driven)

Step 4: Position sizing
  - 2-5% position per activism-adjacent idea
  - Diversify across 5-10 situations (not all campaigns succeed)
  - Exit: When activist premium is fully realized (stock reaches intrinsic value) or when activist exits

Expected return: 15-25% over 6-18 months (activist premium)
Risk: Campaign fails, activist loses proxy fight, or company resists change
```

**The activist premium:**

Academic research and industry data show that stocks targeted by activists outperform the market by 10-20% over the 12 months following the 13D filing. This premium reflects:
- Expected governance improvements
- Capital allocation changes (buybacks, dividends, spin-offs)
- Operational improvements driven by activist pressure
- Potential for sale of the company

**Notable activism-adjacent opportunities:**

| Company | Activist | Outcome |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Yahoo (2016) | Starboard Value | Board seats won; company sold to Verizon |
| ADP (2017) | Elliott Management | Board seats won; operational improvements; stock +40% |
| PepsiCo (2022) | Trian Partners | Board seat won; margin expansion initiatives |
| Disney (2023) | Trian Partners | Pressure led to CEO change; strategic review |

### Activism-Adjacent Checklist

- [ ] **Activist quality:** Activist has a track record of successful campaigns in similar situations
- [ ] **Thesis alignment:** Activist's stated demands are reasonable and value-creating
- [ ] **Shareholder base:** Institutional holders are likely to support activist (ISS/Glass Lewis support probable)
- [ ] **Valuation:** Stock is trading at >20% discount to intrinsic value (activist premium is in addition to this)
- [ ] **Catalyst timeline:** Annual meeting or special meeting within 6-12 months (defined timeline for resolution)
- [ ] **Downside protection:** Even if activist campaign fails, the stock is cheap enough to provide a margin of safety

## Composite Special Situations Scorecard

Use this to evaluate special situations opportunities across all strategies:

| Criterion | Ideal | Acceptable | Avoid |
|-----------|-------|------------|-------|
| Catalyst certainty | Defined event within 6 months | Event within 12 months | Uncertain or >18 months |
| Downside protection | NAV floor, tender offer price, or sum-of-parts floor | 15-20% margin of safety | No defined downside protection |
| Upside potential | 20%+ | 10-20% | <10% |
| Liquidity | High (easy entry/exit) | Moderate | Low (illiquid, hard to exit) |
| Complexity | Simple, easy to explain | Moderate complexity | Highly complex (regulatory, legal) |
| Competition | Few arbitrageurs aware | Moderate competition | Crowded trade (arbitrage spread compressed) |

**Interpretation:**
- 5-6 "Ideal" ratings: High-conviction special situation; size 3-5%
- 3-4 "Ideal" ratings: Moderate conviction; size 1-3%
- 0-2 "Ideal" ratings: Low conviction; avoid or size <1%

> "Special situations are the core of event-driven investing. They are not merger arb, but they share the same DNA: a catalyst, a dislocation, and a reason why the market hasn't arbitraged it away. Our job is to find these situations, size them appropriately, and hold until the catalyst plays out. The rest is patience." — Paulson & Co.
