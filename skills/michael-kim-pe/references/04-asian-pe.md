# Asian PE: Korea, Japan, and China Playbook

> "Asia is not a market. It is a mosaic of distinct cultures, regulatory regimes, and business customs that reward patient students and punish arrogant generalists."
> — MBK Partners investment philosophy

MBK Partners' core competitive advantage is not financial engineering — it is deep, culturally-embedded understanding of three of the world's most complex business environments. This knowledge creates deal flow that global PE firms cannot access and execution advantages they cannot replicate.

## 2. Korea: Chaebol Restructuring and Family Succession

### 2A: Chaebol Non-Core Carve-Outs

**The opportunity:** Korea's chaebol conglomerates (Samsung, Hyundai, SK, LG, Lotte, Hanwha, etc.) built diversified empires over decades of government-directed industrialization. As globalization and governance reform pressure builds, they are continuously shedding non-core subsidiaries.

**Why PE wins these deals:**
- Chaebols want clean, fast exits (no public auction process preferred)
- Strategic buyers often in the same industry (competition concerns)
- PE offers speed, confidentiality, and certainty of close
- MBK's local relationships with chaebol CFOs and strategy teams are a differentiated access point

**Carve-out complexity:**
- **Shared services separation:** IT systems, HR systems, shared manufacturing facilities all intertwined with parent
- **TSA (Transition Services Agreement):** Parent must continue providing services for 12–24 months post-close; negotiate price, scope, and termination rights carefully
- **Workforce:** Korean labor law is strict — cannot reduce headcount freely; restructuring requires union negotiation
- **Branding:** Chaebol subsidiary often carries parent brand; must transition to standalone brand or license agreement

**Valuation discount for carve-outs:**
- Carve-outs should trade at 0.5–2.0x discount to standalone peers due to transition risk, unproven standalone management, and TSA dependency

### 2B: Family Business Succession Deals

**The demographic opportunity:** Korea has ~100,000 companies with revenues > KRW 10B (approximately $7M+) owned by founders born in the 1940s–1960s who are now aging. The Korean inheritance tax rate is the second-highest in the world (maximum 50% on family-owned business assets + additional 20% unlisted company premium). This creates a powerful structural incentive to sell.

**Succession deal genesis:**
1. Founder age 65–75, no compelling successor in family
2. Children often Western-educated, not interested in running the business
3. Korean inheritance tax makes passing the business to heirs extremely expensive
4. Founder prefers to monetize now at fair value, pay taxes cleanly, and retire

**PE approach to family deals:**
- **Source:** Work through Korean business associations, private bankers, law firms (Kim & Chang, Bae Kim & Lee, Yulchon)
- **Trust-building:** The first 6–12 months is relationship-building, not deal-making. Founders do not sell to strangers.
- **Structure:** Founders often want partial liquidity (sell 70–80%, retain 20–30%); structure as majority buyout with founder rollover
- **Management:** Typically hire a professional CEO alongside or replacing the founder; founder becomes chairman (face-saving role)
- **Earnout:** Rarely needed (founder motivated to sell clean); use only if there is a specific performance uncertainty

## 3. Japan: The "Lost Decade" PE Opportunity

### 3A: The Structural Setup

Japan is in a unique moment. After 30 years of corporate stagnation, multiple structural forces are simultaneously creating PE opportunities:

1. **Corporate governance reform:** TSE listing rules now require companies to address below-1.0x PBR businesses; listed companies selling non-core divisions
2. **Aging ownership:** Japanese "salaryman" founder generation retiring; successors often do not exist
3. **Cross-shareholding unwinding:** Corporate groups reducing each other's stock holdings, freeing capital and creating carve-out opportunities
4. **Labor cost pressure:** Despite Japan's traditional lifetime employment model, corporate Japan is opening to restructuring

**MBK's Japan thesis:** Japan has world-class businesses selling at discount to global peers because Japan has historically discouraged PE. This discount is structural but narrowing — meaning returns are best captured now, before multiple expansion normalizes.

### 3B: Navigating Japanese Deal Culture

**Nemawashi (根回し):** The process of building consensus before any formal decision. In Japan, a proposal is never rejected in a meeting — it dies quietly in the pre-meeting process if not properly socialized.

**Key principles for Japan deals:**
- **Time horizon:** First meeting to signed LOI: 12–24 months is normal. 6 months is very fast. Do not rush.
- **Relationship hierarchy:** Access always flows from introduction to introduction. Cold approaches almost never work.
- **Face-saving:** Never create a situation where the Japanese counterparty must say "no" publicly. Always provide dignified alternatives.
- **Language:** All materials in Japanese. English-only presentations signal cultural insensitivity.
- **Local banking:** Megabank (Mizuho, MUFG, SMBC) relationships are essential for debt and market access.

### 3C: Japan-Specific Financial Issues

**Pension liability:** Japanese companies often have significant defined-benefit pension obligations, frequently underfunded. This is a hidden liability that must be fully diligenced and reflected in price.
- Underfunded pension = additional equity check required at close
- Or: negotiate purchase price reduction equal to underfunding

**Lifetime employment:** Reducing headcount in Japan is legally and culturally constrained.
- Voluntary redundancy programs (희망퇴직 equivalent)
- Early retirement packages
- Natural attrition + not replacing departures
- Never forced layoffs of permanent staff without extensive negotiation

**Real estate:** Many Japanese companies own significant real estate (offices, factories) at book value far below market. This is a hidden asset — sale-leaseback can be a significant value creation lever.

## 4. China: Opportunity and Risk Management

### 4A: China's Evolving PE Landscape

**Pre-2021 environment:** China was the most attractive PE market in Asia by growth rate. Consumer internet, healthcare, and education sectors commanded 15–25x EBITDA multiples.

**Post-2021 regulatory reset:** The CCP's "Common Prosperity" campaign fundamentally reset the rules:
- Education sector (tutoring): effectively eliminated by regulation overnight
- Consumer internet: antitrust enforcement reduced valuations 60–90%
- Tech platform: Ant Group IPO blocked; Didi forced to delist
- Real estate: Evergrande crisis; financing restrictions
- VIE structures: legal uncertainty increased substantially

**Current MBK China posture:** Selective and cautious. Prefer sectors with structural demand and limited regulatory risk: healthcare services, industrial services, consumer staples. Avoid: fintech, edtech, consumer internet, real estate.

### 4B: VIE Structure Risks

**What is a VIE (Variable Interest Entity)?**
A legal structure used by Chinese companies to attract foreign investment in sectors restricted to foreign ownership (internet, media, telecom, education). The foreign entity has contractual rights over the Chinese operating company but does not hold equity directly.

**VIE risk taxonomy:**
| Risk | Description | Severity |
|------|-------------|----------|
| Legal enforceability | VIE contracts not tested in courts; PRC law may not enforce | High |
| Regulatory | Government could deem structure illegal (happened: edtech 2021) | High |
| Key person | VIE structure requires founders to sign; founder departure or dispute disruptive | Medium-High |
| Tax | SAFE/CSRC restrictions on capital flows; dividend repatriation | Medium |

**MBK approach:** Avoid VIE-dependent structures for buyout; acceptable for minority where loss of capital is bounded.

### 4C: Cross-Border Regulatory Approvals

**China outbound (Chinese asset → PE ownership):**
- SAMR (State Administration for Market Regulation): Antitrust review; required for significant market positions
- NDRC: Required for certain strategic industries
- MOFCOM: Approval for large-scale transactions

**Korea inbound (foreign acquisition of Korean assets):**
- KFTC: Mandatory for any deal above notification thresholds
- MOTIE: National security-sensitive industries (defense, critical tech, energy)
- Financial Services Commission: Acquisition of financial institutions

**Japan inbound (foreign acquisition of Japanese assets):**
- JFTC: Standard antitrust review
- MoF Foreign Exchange Law: Mandatory prior notification for foreign investors acquiring > 1% of listed companies in certain industries; >10% for most others

**Timeline reality:** Regulatory approvals in Asia routinely take 6–12 months. Build this into deal closing expectations and management retention planning.

## 5. Cross-Border Complexity

### Cultural Integration Risk

PE-backed Asian companies that are sold to or integrated with businesses from a different Asian market face amplified complexity:

**Korea-Japan:** Historical tensions (colonial history); trust requires explicit management. Use neutral third-party facilitators.
**China-Korea:** Close economically; political tensions (THAAD, etc.) create periodic business disruptions.
**Japan-China:** Significant cultural distance; language is a barrier; historical sensitivity.

**Integration principle:** Do not attempt rapid cultural integration. Focus on financial systems integration first; let cultural alignment develop naturally over 12–24 months.

### Currency and FX Risk

| Exposure | Management |
|----------|-----------|
| Korean Won (KRW) | Relatively liquid; forward hedging available |
| Japanese Yen (JPY) | Liquid; yen depreciation creates exit timing considerations |
| Chinese Renminbi (CNY) | Capital controls; repatriation requires regulatory channels; dividend approval from SAFE |
| USD-denominated debt on Asia asset | Natural hedge if revenues are USD; mismatch risk if KRW/JPY revenues |

**MBK practice:** Fund-level currency hedging for major exposures; deal-level hedging for specific large FX events (exit proceeds).
