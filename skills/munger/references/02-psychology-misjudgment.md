# The Psychology of Human Misjudgment

> When to read this file: when analyzing cognitive biases, understanding why people (including investors) make irrational decisions, assessing whether a market or business situation is driven by psychological forces, or identifying "lollapalooza" effects where multiple biases combine.

This is Munger's most original intellectual contribution — a systematic catalog of the ways human psychology leads to predictable errors. First presented as a talk in 1995 at Harvard, later expanded into the famous "25 Standard Causes of Human Misjudgment" chapter in Poor Charlie's Almanack.

> "I went through life and got my ideas from everywhere — from biology, from common sense, from psychology. And the psychology was the most fun. It was just one insight after another, and each seemed so powerful." — 2005 Wesco annual meeting

---

## The 25 Tendencies of Human Misjudgment

### Tendency 1: Reward and Punishment Superresponse

> "Never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives." — Poor Charlie's Almanack

The most powerful tendency of all. Humans respond to incentives with far more intensity than anyone expects — and the response is often **unconscious**. People don't just act in their self-interest; they genuinely believe things that serve their self-interest.

- FedEx couldn't get the night shift to finish on time with hourly pay; switched to per-shift pay and the problem vanished overnight
- Xerox's commission structure accidentally incentivized salespeople to sell the wrong machines
- **In investing:** Sell-side analysts whose firms do investment banking for the company they cover produce systematically biased research — not because they're corrupt, but because incentives warp cognition

**Munger's rule:** "I think I've been in the top 5% of my age cohort all my life in understanding the power of incentives, and all my life I've underestimated it."

### Tendency 2: Liking/Loving

People distort facts and logic to favor those they like or love. They:
- Ignore faults of the loved object
- Favor people, products, and ideas associated with the loved object
- Distort other facts to facilitate love

**In investing:** Falling in love with a stock or a CEO leads to ignoring warning signs. The "halo effect" around a charismatic founder can mask deteriorating fundamentals.

### Tendency 3: Disliking/Hating

The mirror of Tendency 2. People:
- Ignore virtues of the hated object
- Dislike people, products, and ideas associated with the hated object
- Distort other facts to facilitate hatred

**In investing:** Shorting a stock because you "hate" the CEO or the business model, while ignoring genuine competitive strengths.

### Tendency 4: Doubt-Avoidance

The brain tends to quickly remove doubt by reaching a decision — any decision. This is especially powerful under conditions of:
- Stress
- Urgency
- Social pressure

> "The brain of man is programmed with a tendency to quickly remove doubt by reaching some decision." — Poor Charlie's Almanack

**In investing:** The pressure to "do something" when the market drops leads to panic selling — the brain can't tolerate the doubt of "should I hold?"

### Tendency 5: Inconsistency-Avoidance

Once a conclusion is reached, the brain resists changing it. This is the root of:
- **Confirmation bias**: Seeking only information that confirms existing beliefs
- **Commitment escalation**: Continuing to invest in a losing position because changing course would be "inconsistent"
- **Slow updating**: Failing to adjust views when new evidence contradicts the original thesis

> "The human mind is a lot like the human egg, and the human egg has a shut-off device. When one sperm gets in, it shuts down so the next one can't get in. The human mind has a big tendency of the same sort." — 1995 Harvard lecture

**The most dangerous combination:** A public statement of thesis (Tendency 5) combined with social proof from peers who agree (Tendency 10). Once an analyst publicly recommends a stock and their colleagues concur, the analyst becomes psychologically locked in.

### Tendency 6: Curiosity

One of the few positive tendencies. Curiosity drives learning and model accumulation. Munger considers lifelong curiosity essential to good investing and good living.

> "I constantly see people rise in life who are not the smartest, sometimes not even the most diligent, but they are learning machines. They go to bed every night a little wiser than they were when they got up." — 2007 USC commencement

### Tendency 7: Kantian Fairness

Humans expect fair treatment and react intensely to perceived unfairness — even at economic cost to themselves. This drives consumer boycotts, employee strikes, and regulatory backlash against companies perceived as unfair.

**In investing:** Companies that exploit customers during crises (price gouging) often suffer lasting brand damage that exceeds the short-term profit.

### Tendency 8: Envy/Jealousy

> "It is not greed that drives the world, but envy." — Munger at Berkshire 2002 meeting

In investing, envy drives:
- Chasing performance (buying what went up because a neighbor made money)
- Empire-building acquisitions (a CEO whose competitor just made a big acquisition)
- Compensation ratcheting (every CEO must earn more than the peer group median)

### Tendency 9: Reciprocation

Humans feel compelled to reciprocate both favors and injuries. This is exploited commercially through free samples, "free" gifts with purchases, and the investment banking tradition of allocating IPO shares to clients who generate trading commissions.

**In governance:** Board members who owe their positions to the CEO feel obligated to approve the CEO's proposals — destroying board independence.

### Tendency 10: Influence-from-Mere-Association (Pavlovian)

> "The Pavlovian association is an immensely powerful force. It can create and extinguish buying behavior." — 1995 Harvard lecture

People automatically associate:
- High price with high quality (luxury goods)
- Past success with future success (even when circumstances have changed)
- A messenger with the message (shooting the bearer of bad news)

**The "Persian Messenger" syndrome:** Organizations that punish bearers of bad news stop receiving bad news — with catastrophic consequences. This is one of the primary causes of corporate disasters.

**In investing:** A CEO who delivered 15% ROE for 10 years is assumed to continue doing so — even when the competitive landscape has fundamentally changed.

### Tendency 11: Simple, Pain-Avoiding Psychological Denial

When reality is too painful to bear, the mind simply denies it.

> "The mother of a drug-dealing, murdering son will sometimes say, 'He's a good boy. He was just starting to turn his life around.'" — 1995 Harvard lecture

**In investing:** Investors who refuse to sell a catastrophically declining stock because acknowledging the loss is too painful. The loss doesn't become real until you sell — so you never sell. This tendency is enormously destructive to wealth.

### Tendency 12: Excessive Self-Regard

> "We all overrate ourselves." — Munger

People overestimate their own abilities, attractiveness, and judgment. 90% of Swedish drivers rate themselves above average. CEOs systematically believe they can integrate acquisitions better than the base rate of acquisition failure would suggest.

**In investing:** Overconfidence in one's ability to time the market, pick winners, or "see what others don't see." The cure is to look at base rates: what percentage of active managers beat the index over 20 years?

### Tendency 13: Overoptimism

Related to but distinct from self-regard. Humans systematically overestimate the probability of good outcomes and underestimate the probability of bad outcomes.

> "What a man wishes, that also will he believe." — Demosthenes (quoted by Munger)

**In investing:** Projecting the best-case scenario as the "base case" in a financial model. Revenue growth assumptions that have never been achieved in the industry. Management guidance that is systematically optimistic.

### Tendency 14: Deprival-Superreaction

Loss aversion on steroids. People react far more intensely to losing something they already have than to not gaining something they never had.

> "People are really crazy about minor decrements down. And it gets worse with near misses." — 1995 Harvard lecture

**In investing:** This is why selling a position at a loss is so psychologically difficult — it triggers a deprival superreaction. It also explains why companies fight to defend declining businesses long past the point of economic rationality.

### Tendency 15: Social-Proof

> "When people are uncertain, they look at what other people are doing and assume that's the right thing to do." — 1995 Harvard lecture

The driver of market bubbles and panics. When combined with Tendency 4 (doubt avoidance), social proof creates cascading behavior — each person's action provides "proof" to the next person.

**The Kitty Genovese effect:** The more people present, the less likely any individual will act — because each person assumes someone else will handle it. In investing: "If this stock were really mispriced, someone smarter than me would have already bought it."

### Tendency 16: Contrast-Misreaction

Judgments are made relative to nearby contrasts, not on an absolute basis.

> "A person who has just handled a very heavy object will underestimate the weight of a lighter one — even though it's still heavy." — 1995 Harvard lecture

**In investing:** A stock that has fallen from $100 to $50 "looks cheap" relative to $100 — even if its intrinsic value is $30. Real estate brokers exploit this by first showing an overpriced property, making the target property look reasonable by contrast.

### Tendency 17: Stress-Influence

Extreme stress can cause either extreme action (fight-or-flight) or complete shutdown. Both are irrational in most modern contexts.

**In investing:** Market crashes induce stress that leads to panic selling — the exact opposite of rational behavior (which would be buying when prices are depressed).

### Tendency 18: Availability-Misweighing

> "The human brain tends to overweigh what's easily available — what comes to mind easily seems more important than it actually is." — 1995 Harvard lecture

Recent, vivid, emotional information is overweighted. A plane crash makes people afraid to fly even though driving is far more dangerous. A recent stock market crash makes people afraid to invest even when valuations are attractive.

**Munger's antidote:** Checklists. "The main antidote to availability bias is the use of checklists of all the factors that can affect a decision." (See 04-checklist-inversion.md)

### Tendency 19: Use-It-or-Lose-It

Skills and knowledge that are not regularly practiced decay. This applies to analytical skills, foreign languages, and mental models.

**In investing:** An investor who stops reading financial statements for a few years will lose the ability to quickly spot anomalies.

### Tendency 20: Drug-Misinfluence

Included for completeness; directly relevant to analyzing companies in the pharmaceutical, alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis industries.

### Tendency 21: Senescence-Misinfluence

Cognitive decline with age. Relevant when assessing aging founders and CEOs who may be past their peak decision-making ability but refuse to step down (itself a combination of Tendency 5 and Tendency 12).

### Tendency 22: Authority-Misinfluence

> "If the pilot says do something, the co-pilot does it — even if the pilot is clearly wrong." — 1995 Harvard lecture (referencing aviation disasters)

**In corporate settings:** When the CEO speaks, subordinates fall in line — even when the CEO is making a catastrophic mistake. This is a primary driver of the "institutional imperative" that Buffett identified.

### Tendency 23: Twaddle

Talking for the sake of talking, without substance. Munger has zero tolerance for this.

> "I try to get rid of people who always confidently answer questions about which they don't have any real knowledge." — Poor Charlie's Almanack

### Tendency 24: Reason-Respecting

People comply more readily when given a reason — even a meaningless one. "Because" is a magic word.

> "If a professor can explain something, and it makes sense, students will believe it even without evidence. That's both useful and dangerous." — 1995 Harvard lecture

**In investing:** Sell-side research that provides a plausible narrative for a price target is more persuasive than the target alone — even if the narrative is a rationalization.

### Tendency 25: Lollapalooza — The Tendency of Multiple Biases to Combine

> "The most important thing to keep in mind is the idea that especially big forces often come out of these 100 models… When several models combine, you get lollapalooza effects." — 1995 Harvard lecture

**This is Munger's most powerful concept.** When multiple biases operate simultaneously in the same direction, the result is far more extreme than any single bias would produce.

**Classic lollapalooza examples:**
- **Tupperware parties**: Social proof + reciprocation + commitment/consistency + liking = sales rates far above anything achievable through normal retail
- **Open auctions**: Social proof + scarcity + competitive arousal + commitment = prices far above rational value (the "winner's curse")
- **Milgram experiments**: Authority + social proof + commitment/consistency = ordinary people administering what they believe are lethal electric shocks

**In investing — the bubble lollapalooza:**
Social proof (everyone is buying) + overoptimism (prices will keep rising) + envy (my neighbor got rich) + incentive-caused bias (brokers earn commissions on trades) + commitment/consistency (I've already told everyone this is a great investment) = **asset price bubble**

**In investing — the panic lollapalooza:**
Social proof (everyone is selling) + deprival superreaction (I'm losing money) + stress influence (I can't sleep) + availability bias (the last crash is vivid in memory) + doubt avoidance (I need to do something NOW) = **market crash and capitulation at the bottom**

## Practical Application: The Bias Audit

When analyzing any investment situation, run this quick audit:

1. **Incentive check**: Who benefits from this recommendation? What are their incentives?
2. **Social proof check**: Am I buying because others are? Would I buy if no one else was?
3. **Commitment check**: Am I defending a prior decision rather than evaluating fresh evidence?
4. **Contrast check**: Am I anchored to a past price or a comparison that's misleading?
5. **Availability check**: Am I overweighting recent or vivid information?
6. **Lollapalooza check**: Are multiple biases operating in the same direction? If so, the situation is probably more extreme than it appears.

> "The safest way to get what you want is to deserve what you want. The safest way to avoid misjudgment is to know the standard list of misjudgments and check them routinely."
