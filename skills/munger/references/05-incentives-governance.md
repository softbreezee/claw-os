# Incentive Structures, Governance, and Institutional Failure

> When to read this file: when analyzing management compensation structures, assessing board independence, evaluating governance quality, understanding why institutions fail, or diagnosing agency problems.

This is where Munger's psychology meets his investment analysis. The core insight: **incentives control behavior, often unconsciously, and misaligned incentives are the root cause of most corporate disasters.**

> "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." — Munger's most cited aphorism

---

## Incentive-Caused Bias: The Superpower

### The Mechanism

Incentive-caused bias is not just "people do what they're paid to do." It goes far deeper: **people genuinely believe what they're incentivized to believe.** The distortion is cognitive, not merely behavioral.

> "I think I've been in the top 5% of my age cohort all my life in understanding the power of incentives, and all my life I've underestimated it. And never a year passes but I get some surprise that pushes my limit a little farther." — 1995 Harvard lecture

**How the mechanism works:**
1. A person has a financial interest in a particular outcome
2. The subconscious mind filters information to support that outcome
3. The person genuinely believes the filtered conclusion
4. The person acts on this "sincere" belief — making it nearly impossible for outsiders to detect as bias
5. The person would pass a lie detector test because they truly believe what they're saying

### Classic Examples from Munger

**The surgeon who operates:**
> "I once asked a surgeon why he did so many procedures on elderly patients who were unlikely to benefit. He said, 'Well, Charlie, I need the money.'" (paraphrased from various Munger talks)

Most surgeons wouldn't be so candid. They would genuinely believe the surgery was medically necessary — because their incentive to believe it is so powerful.

**The real estate agent:**
Real estate agents consistently believe "now is a good time to buy" — in every market, at every price level. Their commission depends on transactions occurring. They don't consciously lie; they genuinely perceive the market as favorable.

**The auditor:**
> "If you hire an auditor who's trying to keep you as a client, he's going to shade things your way. Not because he's dishonest — he's human. He'll see ambiguous situations in your favor because his income depends on it." — Various Munger talks

This is why Munger considers the structure of the auditing profession fundamentally broken: the auditor is paid by the entity it's supposed to independently evaluate.

## Compensation Structure Analysis

### The Key Questions

When evaluating management compensation, Munger focuses on:

1. **What behavior does the compensation structure reward?**
   - Per-share value growth → Good
   - Revenue growth regardless of profitability → Bad
   - EPS growth (which can be gamed with buybacks funded by debt) → Dangerous
   - Short-term stock price → Very dangerous

2. **Is the downside symmetric with the upside?**
   - Stock options: unlimited upside, zero downside → Misaligned
   - Restricted stock with long vesting: better alignment
   - Personal capital at risk (substantial personal shareholding) → Best alignment

3. **What is the time horizon of the incentive?**
   - Annual bonus → Encourages short-term thinking
   - 3-5 year performance period → Better
   - Permanent ownership (substantial personal net worth in company stock) → Best

> "The compensation committee of a typical corporate board usually consists of directors who are friendly to the CEO. These directors hire compensation consultants who know that pleasing the CEO is how they keep the engagement. The result is a ratcheting-up of executive pay that bears no relationship to performance." — Munger at 2003 Wesco meeting

### The Compensation Ratchet

Munger identified a systemic problem in corporate compensation:

1. Every CEO wants to be paid "above median"
2. Compensation consultants benchmark against peers
3. When one CEO gets a raise, the benchmark shifts up
4. Every other CEO now deserves a raise to stay "above median"
5. The cycle repeats, with compensation spiraling upward regardless of performance

> "The envy/jealousy tendency makes this a one-way ratchet. No CEO wants to be 'below average,' so every adjustment pushes the average higher." — Various Munger talks

### Stock Options: Munger's Specific Critique

> "Stock options are a terrible way to compensate executives. They give all the upside with none of the downside. A CEO who gets options has a free lottery ticket — heads he wins, tails he doesn't lose." — Munger at multiple meetings

Specific problems:
- Options encourage risk-taking (the payoff structure is asymmetric)
- Options encourage short-term stock price manipulation (buybacks timed to option grants)
- Repricing after a decline destroys even the limited alignment that existed
- Options don't require any personal investment — no "skin in the game"

**Munger's preferred alternative:** Large personal shareholding purchased with the executive's own money. "When the CEO has a substantial portion of his net worth in the stock, his interests are genuinely aligned with shareholders."

## Board of Directors: Captured vs. Independent

### The Structural Problem

> "The typical board of directors is a creature of the CEO. The CEO picks the directors, the CEO sets the agenda, the CEO controls the information flow. The 'independent' director depends on the CEO for his board seat and his director's fees. That's not independence — that's captivity." — Munger at Wesco meetings

**Markers of a captured board:**
- Directors were nominated by the CEO (not by shareholders)
- Directors have no significant personal investment in the company
- Board meetings are short, information is pre-digested, and dissent is rare
- The same compensation consultant appears year after year
- Related-party transactions are approved without rigorous scrutiny
- The audit committee has no members with deep accounting expertise

**Markers of a genuinely independent board:**
- Directors have substantial personal net worth invested in the company
- At least some directors were nominated independently of the CEO
- The board has demonstrated willingness to disagree with the CEO publicly
- There is a history of rejecting management proposals
- The audit and compensation committees have genuine expertise and authority

### The Daily Journal Model

Munger's own company, the Daily Journal Corporation, demonstrates his governance philosophy:
- Minimal executive compensation
- Directors held substantial personal positions
- No stock options
- Simple, transparent financial reporting
- Annual meetings that were legendary for candid Q&A

## Institutional Failure: Why Organizations Go Wrong

### The Institutional Imperative (Munger's Extension of Buffett's Concept)

Munger extends Buffett's observation about institutional imperative with a psychological lens:

> "Institutions will tend to do whatever the people inside them are incentivized to do. It doesn't matter what the institution's stated mission is. The behavior follows the incentives, not the mission statement." — Various talks

**The four drivers of institutional failure:**

1. **Incentive misalignment at every level**: From the boardroom to the front line, people optimize for what they're measured on — which is rarely the institution's true purpose.

2. **Information filtering**: Bad news is filtered as it travels up the hierarchy. By the time it reaches the CEO, it's been sanitized. By the time it reaches the board, it's been sanitized twice.

3. **Groupthink and social proof**: Dissent is punished (formally or informally). People learn to go along to get along. The institution develops a "consensus" that may be catastrophically wrong.

4. **Complexity as camouflage**: Complex organizations can hide problems for years. Enron's off-balance-sheet structures, AIG's credit default swaps, and Lehman's Repo 105 transactions all exploited institutional complexity to defer accountability.

### Professional Incentive Corruption

Munger is particularly harsh on professions where the incentive structure systematically corrupts judgment:

**Investment Banking:**
> "Investment bankers will sell you anything. They get paid for deals, not for good deals." — Various talks

**Money Management:**
> "The money management business is a wonderful business — for the managers. It's a terrible business for the customers. The incentive structure guarantees that most active managers will underperform after fees." — 2003 Wesco meeting

The specific problem: managers are paid based on assets under management (AUM), which grows through marketing and asset gathering — not through investment performance. A manager with mediocre returns but great marketing earns more than a manager with excellent returns but no marketing.

**Law:**
> "The ethics of the legal profession are designed by lawyers for lawyers." — Munger, who trained as a lawyer

The billable hour incentive encourages:
- Slow resolution (more hours = more revenue)
- Complexity (simple solutions don't generate fees)
- Litigation over settlement (trials generate more hours than negotiations)

**Accounting:**
The auditing profession's fundamental conflict: the auditor is hired and paid by the entity being audited. Independence is structurally impossible.

> "If you make the auditor dependent on the auditee for his livelihood, you've guaranteed that auditing will have a built-in bias. That's not a theory — it's a certainty." — Various Munger talks

## Regulation and Government Incentive Failures

### Regulatory Capture

> "Every regulator eventually comes to be dominated by the industry it regulates. The industry has concentrated incentives; the public has diffused interests." — Munger at various meetings

The mechanism:
1. Regulated companies have enormous financial interest in favorable regulation
2. They can afford to hire the best lawyers and lobbyists
3. Regulators are often former industry participants (or aspire to be)
4. The "public interest" is abstract and has no organized lobby
5. Over time, regulation shifts from protecting the public to protecting incumbents

### The Revolving Door

Industry executives become regulators, then return to industry at higher salaries. This creates:
- Reluctance to antagonize future employers
- Sympathetic interpretation of regulations
- Information asymmetry (industry knows more than regulators)

## Practical Applications: The Incentive Audit

When analyzing any company, institution, or recommendation, run this audit:

### For Management:
1. How much of management's personal wealth is in company stock?
2. Is compensation tied to per-share value creation or raw growth metrics?
3. Does the compensation structure have meaningful downside (not just reduced upside)?
4. Has management ever voluntarily reduced their compensation?
5. How does management treat errors — hiding or disclosing?

### For Boards:
1. How were directors selected? By management or by shareholders?
2. What is the total dollar value of each director's personal holdings?
3. Has the board ever publicly disagreed with management?
4. Are audit and compensation committees staffed with genuine experts?
5. Are there related-party transactions that require scrutiny?

### For Recommendations:
1. Who is making this recommendation?
2. How do they get paid?
3. What happens to them if I follow their advice and it fails?
4. What would they recommend if their incentives were different?
5. "Whose bread I eat, his song I sing" — whose bread is this person eating?

### For Institutions:
1. What is the stated mission?
2. What are the actual incentive structures?
3. Where the mission and incentives conflict, which wins? (Answer: always incentives)
4. Is information flowing freely up the hierarchy, or being filtered?
5. What would a hostile investigator find?

> "I'd rather have a termite inspector than a stock inspector. The termite inspector is looking for termites, and he finds them. The stock inspector — or the auditor — he's looking for problems, but he's paid by the guy who might have the problems." — Munger
