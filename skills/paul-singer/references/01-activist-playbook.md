# The Elliott Activist Playbook: Five Campaign Archetypes

> **When to read this file**: when designing an activist campaign, evaluating which Elliott tool fits a specific situation, assessing the probability of success for a given campaign type, or understanding how Elliott escalates from "constructive" to "hostile."

---

## Core Philosophy: Activism as Value Engineering

Elliott's activism is not primarily about attacking management — it's about identifying situations where a specific, executable action will close a verifiable value gap. The campaign is the mechanism; closing the gap is the goal.

> "We are not corporate raiders. We don't want to take companies over and run them. We want to catalyze companies into taking actions that create shareholder value that should have been created anyway." — Paul Singer, 2018

> "There is a tendency to view activist investors as short-term oriented, as people who don't understand the business. We understand the businesses extremely well. We've done the work." — Jesse Cohn, Elliott Portfolio Manager

**The Iron Rule of Elliott Activism**: Never launch a campaign without a specific, quantified demand. "Unlock value" is not a demand. "Separate the $4B Consumer Products division through a tax-free spin-off at peer-group EBITDA multiples of 12-14x, creating ~40% upside to current share price by Q3 2025" is a demand.

## The Five Campaign Archetypes

### Archetype 1: Board Seat Acquisition

**Root cause it addresses**: Governance failure — board that is captured, unqualified, or insufficiently independent to hold management accountable.

**Mechanics**:
- Elliott nominates 1-3 director candidates with specific operational or financial expertise
- Seeks director election at annual meeting or via written consent (if permitted)
- Directors serve as "eyes and ears" on the board — not as operators
- Board seats create ongoing leverage for all other demands

**When to use**:
- Board has ≥3 directors who have served >10 years with poor stock performance
- Board has personal/financial relationships with CEO compromising independence
- Previous engagement has been rebuffed without substantive response
- The target's other shareholders lack meaningful board representation

**Success rate drivers** (based on Elliott campaign analysis):
- ISS/Glass Lewis support → win rate jumps from ~50% to ~85%
- Large institutional co-investors (>20% supporting) → +25% success rate
- CEO with below-median TSR over 3+ years → +20% success rate
- Staggered board: reduces win probability by ~30% but often forces settlement

**Escalation ladder**:
1. Private engagement → request board seats informally
2. Formal nomination (sets SEC/exchange timelines)
3. Public letter + investor presentations
4. Proxy solicitation (engage Innisfree, MacKenzie, Okapi)
5. Withhold campaign against existing directors

**Settlement patterns**: ~65% of board seat campaigns settle before the annual meeting vote, typically with Elliott receiving 1-2 seats and a commitment to explore strategic alternatives.

**Key metric**: Demand board seats when the ISS pre-assessment score (governance quality) is below the median for the sector.

### Archetype 2: Strategic Review / Strategic Alternatives

**Root cause it addresses**: Strategic drift — management pursuing growth in non-core areas, destroying value through misallocation, or ignoring a compelling exit/merger opportunity.

**Mechanics**:
- Elliott demands that the board formally engage investment bankers to conduct a "strategic review"
- Review encompasses: sale of whole company, sale of divisions, spin-offs, mergers of equals, partnership structures
- The demand itself creates option value — often triggers bidders to surface
- If management refuses, Elliott escalates to proxy fight or hostile tender

**When to use**:
- Sum-of-parts analysis shows ≥30% discount to whole-company valuation
- Company has recently made a value-destroying acquisition (defense acquisitions are particularly targeted)
- Peer companies have been acquired at meaningful premiums while target sits flat
- CEO succession is imminent — optimal timing for "fresh look" demands

**Favorite Elliott targets for this campaign type**:
- Diversified industrials with unrelated divisions
- Technology companies that made large software acquisitions at peak multiples
- Healthcare conglomerates where the pharma/device mix creates discount
- Telecoms/media with both regulated and unregulated assets

**Timeline**: 12-30 months from first public engagement to announced transaction or clear rejection.

**Success metrics**: A successful strategic review campaign results in announced sale/spin/merger within 24 months. Partial success: spin-off of one division even if whole-company sale doesn't occur.

**Key insight from Elliott campaigns**: Simply demanding a strategic review — even if rejected — creates 10-15% stock appreciation on average as the market reprices the optionality.

### Archetype 3: Spin-Off / Separation Demand

**Root cause it addresses**: Conglomerate discount — the market values the whole company at a discount to the sum of its independently-valued parts, and separation would eliminate the discount and allow management teams to focus.

**The Spin-Off Playbook**:

**Step 1 — Identify the "crown jewel"**: Which division, if separated, would trade at a significantly higher multiple as a pure-play? Look for:
- High-growth tech division inside an industrial conglomerate
- Fast-growing consumer brand inside a B2B company
- Recurring software revenue inside a hardware business

**Step 2 — Build the sum-of-parts model**:
```
Division A (core, valued at industry EBITDA multiple): $X
Division B (high-growth, valued at pure-play multiple): $Y
Division C (subscale, valued at liquidation or peer buyout): $Z
Less: Corporate overhead (capitalize at 10-12x): $(W)
Less: Net debt: $(D)
= Sum-of-Parts equity value: $SOP

Current market cap: $M
Value gap: ($SOP - $M) / $M = XX%
```

**Step 3 — Propose the structure**:
- Tax-free Section 355 spin-off (preferred — no tax leakage)
- Or tax-efficient Morris Trust / Reverse Morris Trust for cash proceeds
- Pro forma capital structure for each entity
- Management team for each entity (who runs the SpinCo?)

**Step 4 — Quantify the "spin-off premium"**:
Academic research: spin-offs outperform by 10-20% in years 1-3 vs. market.
Elliott's experience: pure-play focused management + appropriate capital structure + right investor base = sustained re-rating.

**Typical timeline**: 18-36 months from proposal to completed transaction (regulatory, tax structuring, operational separation take time).

**When spin-offs fail**: If the two businesses are deeply intertwined operationally (shared manufacturing, shared customer relationships), separation costs can eat the value gap. Elliott models these costs explicitly.

### Archetype 4: Capital Return / Capital Structure Optimization

**Root cause it addresses**: Capital structure inefficiency — company holds excess cash, uses sub-optimal leverage, and returns insufficient capital to shareholders while sitting on an underperforming balance sheet.

**This is Elliott's highest-success-rate campaign type (~80% success rate)** because:
1. The math is unambiguous and easily communicated to other shareholders
2. It does not require management change (less existential threat to CEO)
3. The outcome is visible and verifiable
4. ISS and Glass Lewis are generally supportive of disciplined capital returns

**The Capital Return Demand Framework**:

**A. Buyback Acceleration**:
- Identify excess cash (cash > 3 months of operating expenses)
- Model accelerated buyback (e.g., $3B buyback over 18 months vs. current $1B/year pace)
- Calculate EPS accretion at current P/E
- Demand: Specific dollar amount + specific timeline + specific commitment

**B. Special Dividend**:
- Used when management has structural aversion to buybacks (e.g., family-controlled boards prefer dividends)
- Sized at 10-20% of market cap (large enough to matter, small enough to not impair the business)
- One-time vs. recurring special dividend (recurring preferred as it creates ongoing discipline)

**C. Leveraged Recapitalization**:
- Issue debt at investment-grade rates; use proceeds to buy back stock
- Optimal when: Stock trades at >10x P/FCF, company has below-median leverage, interest coverage >5x
- Value creation mechanism: Debt is tax-deductible; equity is not. At 30% tax rate, $1B of new debt creates ~$300M of PV tax shield.
- Model: [Pre-recap EV] minus [New debt] = [Post-recap equity] ÷ [Fewer shares] = [Higher per-share value]

**D. Dividend Initiation**:
- For companies with high FCF and no dividend — demand dividend initiation as signal of capital discipline
- Creates a permanent commitment mechanism that constrains future value-destructive M&A

**Communication template**: Elliott typically packages capital structure demands in a public letter with:
- Side-by-side: "Current trajectory" vs. "Proposed capital structure"
- 3-year IRR comparison for shareholders
- Peer leverage comparison
- Management acknowledgment that operations are not impaired by the new structure

### Archetype 5: Management / CEO Change

**Root cause it addresses**: Leadership failure — the CEO has fundamentally misallocated capital, destroyed competitive position, or demonstrated inability to execute, and no amount of board oversight or capital structure change will fix the underlying problem.

**Elliott's rule on CEO campaigns**: This is the highest-risk archetype. Attacking a CEO personally:
- Creates institutional opposition (other CEOs, business press sympathy)
- Makes settlement harder (management entrenches when existentially threatened)
- Requires naming a credible replacement before going public

**The CEO Change Playbook**:
1. **Build the record**: Document every capital allocation error, missed guidance, value-destroying M&A with specifics and dates
2. **Identify the successor**: Approach a specific candidate (internal or external) before going public — "change to whom?" is the first question analysts ask
3. **Soften the ask publicly**: Frame as "succession planning" or "strategic leadership review" rather than "fire the CEO"
4. **Use board seats as leverage**: Get seats first, let directors make the case internally before public confrontation
5. **Build institutional support**: Ensure ≥25% of the shareholder base supports the thesis before going public

**Typical CEO change timeline**: 24-48 months — the longest of any campaign type.

**When NOT to demand CEO change**:
- CEO is a founder with >20% ownership (founder control = almost impossible without their consent)
- Recent CEO hire (<18 months into tenure) — market gives new management the benefit of the doubt
- Operations are improving — campaigns during operational turnaround lose ISS support

## The "Constructive to Hostile" Transition Framework

Elliott almost always begins constructively. The decision to "go hostile" is calibrated:

```
Phase 1 — Quiet engagement (0-6 months):
  → Private letters, management meetings, board requests
  → Assessment: Is management listening? Are they making changes?
  → Test: Is there good faith dialogue, or delay tactics?

Phase 2 — Public announcement of position (6-12 months if no progress):
  → 13D filing triggers public campaign
  → Open letter to board with specific demands
  → Investor presentation distributed to institutional holders
  → Assessment: Did management respond to public pressure?

Phase 3 — Escalation (12-24 months if demands unmet):
  → Director nominations
  → Proxy solicitation
  → Litigation (books and records, appraisal rights)
  → Strategic: Approach potential acquirers/merger partners

Phase 4 — Settlement or proxy contest (24-36 months):
  → Most campaigns settle in Phase 2-3
  → ~15% of campaigns reach actual proxy fight
  → Elliott wins proxy fights at ~70% rate when they proceed to a vote
```

**The Key Leverage Insight**: The mere credibility of Elliott's escalation willingness is what makes settlements happen. Management knows Elliott will spend the money, has the lawyers, and has won before. The threat is credible.

## Campaign Success Rate Summary

| Campaign Type | Estimated Success Rate | Average Duration | Key Success Driver |
|--------------|----------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Capital Return | ~80% | 6-18 months | Math is unambiguous; low CEO threat |
| Block/Reverse M&A | ~75% | 3-12 months | Time pressure; binary outcome |
| Board Seats | ~70% | 12-24 months | ISS support is pivotal |
| Strategic Review / Spin-off | ~65% | 18-30 months | Requires specific buyer/structure |
| Management Change | ~55% | 24-48 months | Most resistance; hardest to execute |

**The Meta-Lesson**: Elliott rarely uses a single archetype. Most campaigns combine 2-3 types (e.g., board seats + strategic review + capital return). The combination creates multiple simultaneous pressure points that are harder for management to defuse simultaneously.

## Pre-Campaign Checklist

Before launching any campaign, Elliott verifies:

- [ ] Value gap ≥25% of current market cap (quantified, not estimated)
- [ ] Root cause is identifiable and addressable within 36 months
- [ ] Ownership position accumulated without tipping disclosure thresholds prematurely
- [ ] Institutional shareholder base analyzed — who else is frustrated?
- [ ] ISS governance score obtained — will ISS likely support our slate?
- [ ] Legal preparation complete (proxy rules, nomination deadlines, litigation options)
- [ ] PR/IR firm retained (Joele Frank or similar)
- [ ] Proxy solicitor engaged (Innisfree, MacKenzie, or Okapi)
- [ ] Candidate directors identified and pre-cleared (if board seat campaign)
- [ ] Successor CEO identified and willing (if management change campaign)
- [ ] Hedges in place to neutralize market/sector exposure of the position
