# Elliott Campaign Case Studies: 10 Major Campaigns Analyzed

> **When to read this file**: when pattern-matching a current situation against Elliott's historical campaigns, identifying which prior campaign is most structurally analogous, extracting lessons from both successful and unsuccessful interventions, or understanding how Elliott's approach has evolved over 47 years.

---

## How to Use This File

For each new situation, identify which 1-2 case studies are most structurally similar. Ask:
1. What was the root cause in the historical case?
2. What campaign type did Elliott use?
3. What was the catalyst that finally closed the value gap?
4. What nearly derailed the campaign?

The pattern library is more valuable than any single case study.

## Case Study 1: Twitter / X (2022)

**Elliott's involvement**: Accumulated ~11% stake (~$1B+) in early 2020; board seats obtained; later re-emerged in 2022 Musk acquisition saga.

**Root cause**: CEO distraction (Jack Dorsey's divided attention between Twitter and Square), chronically below-peer revenue growth despite leading social platform, persistent operational underperformance vs. Snap, Pinterest, and Facebook.

**Campaign type**: Board seats + Strategic review + CEO change.

**What Elliott did**:
- Accumulated 11% stake quietly; disclosed just before engagement
- Nominated 4 directors with clear tech/ops credentials
- Demanded Dorsey commit to full-time role or step aside
- Simultaneously explored strategic alternatives (sale of company)
- Settled for 3 board seats + commitment to operational improvement plan

**What actually closed the gap**: Elon Musk's hostile bid at $54.20/share (April 2022) — 38% premium to unaffected price. Elliott's board engagement had already established that Twitter was a strategic asset willing to engage with suitors.

**Lessons extracted**:
- CEO dual role is an activist red flag: founder/CEO running two public companies creates perfect governance failure target
- Platform companies with below-peer monetization (RPU) are particularly vulnerable to "you're leaving money on the table" campaigns
- Strategic review pressure often surfaces unexpected buyers — Elliott's work made Twitter "known to be in play"
- The final value was created by a buyer, not by operational improvement — activist thesis doesn't require operations to fix themselves

**Pattern**: Tech platform + founder distraction + monetization gap + strategic value to acquirers = canonical Elliott target.

## Case Study 2: AT&T (2019-2022)

**Elliott's involvement**: $3.2B stake disclosed in September 2019 — one of Elliott's largest disclosed positions.

**Root cause**: WarnerMedia acquisition ($85B, 2018) destroyed capital; DirecTV acquisition ($67B, 2015) was legacy media bet at peak; leverage ballooned to >3.5x EBITDA; telecom core underperformed Verizon on margins and network investment.

**Campaign type**: Capital structure + Strategic review + CEO/management change.

**What Elliott did**:
- Published detailed 24-page public letter quantifying the value destruction
- Proposed specific asset divestitures (DirecTV, WarnerMedia)
- Demanded CEO succession planning (Randall Stephenson)
- Calculated $60/share value vs. ~$37 current price = 60% upside
- Built coalition with Vanguard, BlackRock (collectively frustrated with M&A track record)

**Outcomes**:
- Stephenson announced retirement (July 2020 — Elliott's key ask)
- WarnerMedia spun off and merged with Discovery (announced May 2021)
- DirecTV spun off with TPG (announced February 2021)
- AT&T resumed focus on telecom core

**What didn't work**: AT&T stock remained depressed post-spin due to dividend cut and secular challenges in the core telecom business. Elliott's $60 price target was not achieved even after all demanded actions were completed.

**Lessons extracted**:
- Even "successful" campaigns can produce disappointing absolute returns if the industry has structural headwinds
- Conglomerate unwinding takes longer than expected — WarnerMedia took 2.5 years
- CEO change achieved through succession planning framing (not hostile) — "constructive" approach preserved relationships
- At $3.2B, position size required careful liquidity management

**Pattern**: Mega-cap conglomerate + M&A-driven leverage + below-peer core margins + CEO succession opportunity = capital return + unwinding campaign.

## Case Study 3: SoftBank (2020)

**Elliott's involvement**: ~$2.5B stake in SoftBank Group Corp. (Tokyo-listed), disclosed February 2020.

**Root cause**: SoftBank traded at 50-60% discount to stated NAV due to complex holding structure, WeWork implosion (Oct 2019), opaque Vision Fund losses, and Masa Son's idiosyncratic investment style creating market skepticism about governance.

**Campaign type**: Capital return + Governance improvement + Transparency demand.

**What Elliott did**:
- Identified NAV discount as the primary lever — Alibaba stake alone worth more than the entire SoftBank market cap
- Demanded ¥2 trillion ($18B) buyback — the largest in Japanese corporate history
- Pushed for enhanced disclosure of Vision Fund holdings and performance
- Leveraged Japan's evolving corporate governance reform environment (JPX stewardship code)

**Outcomes**:
- SoftBank announced ¥4.5 trillion ($41B) asset sale + buyback program (March 2020) — larger than Elliott demanded
- NAV discount compressed from 50%+ to ~30%
- SoftBank stock +50% from Elliott's entry to announcement

**Complications**: COVID-19 hit during the campaign; SoftBank's execution of asset sales was partly forced by market conditions, not purely by Elliott's pressure.

**Lessons extracted**:
- Discount-to-NAV situations in complex holding companies are "pure math" campaigns — hardest to resist politically
- Japanese activism requires understanding cultural dynamics: "helping management" framing is essential in Japan
- Regulatory tailwinds (Japan's governance reform push) amplify activist leverage — know the regulatory environment
- Masa Son's quirky communication style made "transparency demand" a highly resonant public campaign

**Pattern**: Holding company conglomerate discount + opaque structure + large liquid asset (Alibaba) + governance reform tailwind = NAV arbitrage campaign.

## Case Study 4: Samsung Electronics (2016-2017)

**Elliott's involvement**: Acquired ~1% stake; blocked Cheil/Samsung merger (2015); later advocated for restructuring.

**Root cause**: Samsung Group's circular cross-shareholding structure entrapped value across multiple entities; Lee family used the corporate structure for inheritance/control at the expense of minority shareholders; Samsung Electronics traded at massive discount to global tech peers (Apple, TSMC).

**Campaign type**: Governance reform + Capital return + Structural simplification.

**What Elliott did**:
- Proposed splitting Samsung Electronics into operating company + holding company
- Demanded $27B special dividend and buyback
- Argued the Lee family structure imposed ~30% "Korea discount" on shares
- Engaged Korean institutional investors and pension funds (NPS)
- Filed detailed proxy materials in Korean (unusual for a foreign activist)

**Outcomes** (mixed):
- Samsung announced $35B buyback — exceeding Elliott's demand
- Holding company split was NOT executed — Korean government, NPS, and management blocked it
- Lee Jae-yong bribery scandal disrupted everything (February 2017 arrest)
- Partial success: massive capital return but structural reform deferred

**Lessons extracted**:
- Country-level governance risk (chaebol structure, government influence) can block even meritorious activist demands
- Building coalitions with domestic institutions (NPS) is essential for foreign activists in Korea/Japan
- Geopolitical factors (semiconductor national security) reduce activist leverage in strategic industries
- Capital return demands succeed even when structural demands fail — a winnable battle within a partially lost war

**Pattern**: Chaebol / family-controlled conglomerate + circular ownership structure + massive discount to global peers = challenging campaign requiring domestic coalition.

## Case Study 5: Hess Corporation (2013)

**Elliott's involvement**: ~5% stake; full proxy fight; won 3 board seats.

**Root cause**: Hess was an energy conglomerate (E&P + refining + terminals + retail gas stations + Hovensa JV) that the market had de-rated due to strategic incoherence. Pure-play E&P peers traded at 50%+ premium.

**Campaign type**: Strategic review + Spin-off + Board seats + Management change (partial).

**What Elliott did**:
- Built detailed asset-by-asset sum-of-parts showing $126/share intrinsic value vs. ~$70 market price
- Demanded separation into pure-play E&P + divestiture of non-core assets
- Ran full proxy fight — won 3 of 5 seats contested despite Hess family opposition
- John Hess (founder's son as CEO) was the most significant resistance factor

**Outcomes**:
- Hess exited retail gas stations (sold to Marathon for $2.6B)
- Hess exited refining (Hovensa joint venture dissolved)
- Hess exited terminals (sold to Global Partners)
- Stock re-rated from $70 to >$90 on pure-play E&P premium
- Elliott's 3 board seats were the decisive leverage

**Lessons extracted**:
- Even with founding family opposition, a full proxy fight is winnable with 70%+ institutional ownership
- Energy sector sum-of-parts analysis (E&P vs. downstream vs. midstream) creates very visible value gaps
- Proxy victory doesn't require majority control — 3/10 board seats materially changes management behavior
- "Strategic incoherence" is a particularly resonant activist message — even non-activist investors understand it

**Pattern**: Energy conglomerate + family CEO with founder's aura but poor returns + 100% institutional floatable stock + clear asset-by-asset value = winnable proxy fight.

## Case Study 6: SAP (2023)

**Elliott's involvement**: ~$1B+ stake disclosed June 2023; engagement with management over operational efficiency.

**Root cause**: SAP's cloud transition had depressed near-term margins while revenue from legacy ERP maintenance was declining; operating margins were 20-22% vs. SaaS peers at 25-35%; "total cost of ownership" program promised $350M savings but lacked urgency.

**Campaign type**: Operational improvement + Capital return (indirect).

**What Elliott did**:
- Published investment thesis on SAP's cloud transformation opportunity
- Quantified the margin gap vs. pure-play cloud peers (Salesforce, Workday)
- Pushed for acceleration of the "RISE with SAP" cloud migration
- Engaged with management privately (no proxy fight — cooperative campaign)

**Outcomes**:
- SAP announced major restructuring (January 2024): 8,000 job cuts (6% of workforce)
- Margin expansion target raised; focus narrowed to core ERP cloud
- Stock +60% from Elliott's disclosure to end-2024

**Lessons extracted**:
- Cooperative campaigns can produce superior outcomes without the legal/PR cost of proxy fights
- European technology companies are increasingly receptive to US-style activist pressure
- "Operational efficiency" framing is less threatening than "capital structure" demands — management can execute without feeling existentially threatened
- Cloud transition "J-curve" creates temporary margin depression that activists can exploit if they trust the underlying business

**Pattern**: Enterprise software + cloud transition margin trough + clear peer benchmark for margin improvement = operational improvement campaign without governance confrontation.

## Case Study 7: Citrix Systems (2015, 2021)

**Elliott's involvement**: Two separate interventions; 2015: pushed for sale; 2021: pushed for go-private with Vista Equity.

**Root cause**: Citrix's core virtualization/VDI products were mature; cloud competition from Microsoft (Azure Virtual Desktop) was accelerating; management was investing in adjacencies (Podio, ShareFile) that the market did not value; stock chronically underperformed vs. software peers.

**Campaign type**: Strategic review + Sale of company + Private equity partnership.

**What Elliott did**:
- 2015: Demanded strategic alternatives; Citrix sold enterprise mobility division and bought back stock
- 2021: Partnered with Vista Equity (PE buyout specialist) to propose go-private at $104/share
- Leveraged Vista's software operating expertise as "value-add" credential
- Final deal closed at $104/share (2022)

**Lessons extracted**:
- Persistent activists can run multiple campaigns at the same company over years
- PE partnership (activist + PE sponsor) is a powerful combination: activist provides exit pressure, PE provides credible acquirer
- Software companies with "product life cycle maturity" are ideal LBO targets: predictable cash flows, high customer switching costs, margin expansion potential
- Go-private eliminates the short-termism vs. long-term investment tension that public markets create for transition-stage software

**Pattern**: Mature enterprise software + cloud competition + market skepticism on transition + PE-friendly cash flow profile = go-private campaign.

## Case Study 8: Pinterest (2022)

**Elliott's involvement**: Disclosed ~9% stake (largest shareholder) in October 2022.

**Root cause**: Post-COVID user decline (-9% MAU in 2022); stock down 75% from 2021 peak; monetization per user well below Instagram/Snap peers; CEO Ben Silbermann announced departure (transitioning to executive chairman) — succession risk.

**Campaign type**: CEO succession support + Strategic alternatives monitoring.

**What Elliott did**:
- Accumulated 9% stake during the selloff (bought the blood)
- Signaled support for CEO succession process (Bill Ready hired from Google Commerce)
- Pushed for focus on e-commerce monetization (Pinterest's natural product adjacency)
- Acted as "supportive major shareholder" — less hostile than typical Elliott

**Outcomes**:
- Bill Ready (new CEO) delivered ARPU improvement and cost discipline
- Stock recovered from ~$18 to ~$35 by late 2023
- Elliott did not engage in proxy fight — constructive role delivered returns

**Lessons extracted**:
- CEO transition moments create opportunity to get in at maximum uncertainty
- Not every campaign requires confrontation — being the supportive "adult in the room" during a crisis can be equally profitable
- E-commerce monetization unlock is a thesis that resonates with other investors and boards alike
- Social media platforms with below-peer ARPU are structural activist targets

**Pattern**: Consumer tech platform + post-hypergrowth reset + CEO succession + clear monetization gap = constructive accumulation opportunity without confrontation.

## Case Study 9: NorTel Networks / Arconic (2016-2017)

**Elliott's involvement**: Pushed for Klaus Kleinfeld's ouster as CEO of Alcoa's aerospace/auto parts spinoff Arconic.

**Root cause**: Arconic was spun off from Alcoa (November 2016) with Klaus Kleinfeld as CEO; Elliott believed Kleinfeld's Alcoa strategy had destroyed value and the new Arconic needed fresh leadership to realize aerospace margins.

**Campaign type**: CEO change + Operational improvement post-spin.

**What Elliott did**:
- Disclosed 9.4% stake in Arconic (January 2017 — within 2 months of spin-off)
- Ran formal proxy fight nominating 4 directors
- Kleinfeld sent an unusual personal letter to Singer — used as evidence of poor judgment
- Elliott won the proxy fight: Kleinfeld resigned (April 2017)

**Lessons extracted**:
- Spin-offs often create "fresh start" governance situations that are ideal for activist intervention
- A CEO who cannot manage an activist professionally is disqualifying — the Kleinfeld letter accelerated Elliott's win
- Strike within the first 6 months of a spin-off: the company lacks defensive entrenchment, the board is new, and ISS has no loyalty to management
- Aerospace/defense segments in industrials typically trade at premium multiples — post-Kleinfeld Arconic re-rated toward those peers

**Pattern**: Recent spin-off + CEO credibility deficit + industrial-to-aerospace re-rating opportunity = immediate post-spin activist intervention.

## Case Study 10: BHP (2021-2022)

**Elliott's involvement**: Long-running engagement (since 2017); ultimately successful in 2022 with unification of dual-listed structure.

**Root cause**: BHP had a dual-listed company (DLC) structure with separate London (BHP Group PLC) and Australian (BHP Group Ltd) shares. The UK shares traded at persistent 10-15% discount to Australian shares due to FTSE index mechanics. Oil & gas assets were non-core at a moment when ESG pressure was building.

**Campaign type**: Structural simplification + Strategic review (petroleum divestiture).

**What Elliott did**:
- Calculated precise UK vs. Australian share discount
- Proposed collapsing the DLC structure to eliminate the discount
- Proposed spinning off or selling petroleum assets (which were weighing down ESG ratings)
- Engaged with both UK and Australian institutional shareholders

**Outcomes**:
- BHP collapsed DLC structure (January 2022) — UK shares unified with Australian
- BHP divested petroleum assets to Woodside (merged, received Woodside shares)
- Multi-year campaign but ultimately achieved both demands

**Lessons extracted**:
- Multi-year activist campaigns are viable when the thesis is unambiguous (DLC discount is pure math)
- ESG framework changes (stranded asset risk in oil) provided unexpected tailwind for Elliott's petroleum divestiture demand
- Cross-border campaigns require engaging two separate regulatory and institutional shareholder environments
- Structural complexity (DLC) creates investor confusion that depresses price — simplification itself creates value

**Pattern**: Structural complexity discount + non-core asset ESG liability + two-jurisdiction shareholder base = multi-year structural simplification campaign.

## Pattern Extraction: What Makes an Elliott Campaign Work

**The Five Common Success Factors**:
1. **Quantifiable gap**: The value gap must be expressible in $ per share. "Management is bad" is not a thesis; "$14/share of value is trapped in the Consumer division at 9x EBITDA vs. peer 13x" is a thesis.
2. **Addressable root cause**: The cause of the gap must be fixable within 24-36 months by actions a board can take (sell division, buy back stock, change CEO).
3. **Supportable by ISS/major institutions**: Elliott cannot win alone. The thesis must resonate with Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street — who collectively own 15-25% of most large-cap targets.
4. **Low operational risk**: Elliott prefers situations where the underlying business is structurally sound — the value gap is governance/structure, not business quality.
5. **Defined exit**: Elliott always enters with a view of how the position exits — sale, buyback, special dividend, re-rating after spin. "Hold forever and hope" is not an Elliott strategy.

**The Three Most Common Failure Modes**:
1. **Founder/family control**: Elliott cannot win when a controlling family (founder with 40%+ voting power) blocks every demand. Samsung is the canonical example.
2. **Macro headwinds**: Even winning the campaign doesn't guarantee returns if the industry has structural decline (see AT&T's post-spin telecom performance).
3. **Execution risk underestimated**: Spin-offs and mergers take 2-3x longer than expected and are more operationally disruptive than the thesis assumes.
