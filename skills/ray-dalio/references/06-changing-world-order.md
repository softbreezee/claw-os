# The Changing World Order

> When to read this file: when analyzing geopolitical risks, when evaluating the long-term trajectory of nations and empires, when assessing reserve currency risks, or when studying the rise of China and the relative position of the United States.

Based on Dalio's 2021 book "Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order," which studies the rise and decline of the Dutch, British, American, and Chinese empires over 500 years.

> "If you don't study history, you'll make the same mistakes that have been made many times before." — Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order

---

## The Big Cycle of Empires

### The Pattern

Dalio identifies a recurring pattern that all great empires follow:

**Rise (50-100 years):**
1. Strong education → innovative and productive workforce
2. Innovation → new technologies and industries
3. Competitiveness → growing share of world trade
4. Military strength → ability to project power and protect trade
5. Financial center status → capital flows in, currency strengthens
6. Reserve currency status → borrowing becomes easy and cheap

**Peak (20-40 years):**
1. Wealth creates complacency
2. Productivity advantage erodes (competitors catch up)
3. Internal inequality grows (winners vs. losers from globalization)
4. Military spending becomes burdensome
5. Debt grows (easy to borrow in reserve currency)
6. Political polarization increases

**Decline (50-100 years):**
1. Competitiveness fades → trade deficits grow
2. Debt becomes unsustainable → printing money → currency debasement
3. Internal conflict intensifies (wealth gap, political polarization)
4. External challenges from rising powers
5. Reserve currency status is questioned → capital outflows
6. Military overstretches

### The Eight Key Measures of Power

Dalio tracks eight dimensions to assess where a country stands:

1. **Education**: Quality of education system, STEM graduation rates
2. **Innovation/Technology**: R&D spending, patents, tech companies
3. **Competitiveness**: Cost competitiveness, export market share
4. **Military**: Spending, capability, alliance network
5. **Trade**: Current account, trade volume, terms of trade
6. **Economic Output**: GDP, GDP growth, productivity
7. **Financial Center**: Capital market depth, reserve currency status
8. **Reserve Currency Status**: Share of global reserves, usage in trade

## The US-China Dynamic

### Where Each Stands

**United States (late empire, still dominant but declining in relative terms):**
- Still #1 in innovation, military, financial center, reserve currency
- Declining in: education quality (relative to peers), competitiveness, fiscal health
- High internal conflict: political polarization, wealth inequality, social division
- High external debt: $35T+ national debt, largely funded by foreign holders

**China (rising empire, challenging but not yet dominant):**
- Rising rapidly in: education (especially STEM), innovation, economic output, military
- Weaker in: reserve currency status, financial market depth, rule of law for capital
- Lower internal conflict currently but authoritarian structure creates different risks
- Key advantage: long-term planning capability (vs. democratic short-term cycles)

> "The United States and China are in a great power competition that will be the most important dynamic of our lifetimes." — Dalio

## Reserve Currency Dynamics

### Why Reserve Currency Status Matters

The reserve currency is the world's preferred medium of exchange and store of value. The country that issues it enjoys enormous advantages:

1. **Ability to borrow cheaply**: Everyone wants to hold your currency → willing to lend at low rates
2. **Ability to print money**: When you print, the world absorbs the inflation (not just domestic)
3. **Sanctions power**: You can cut adversaries off from the global financial system
4. **Seigniorage**: You earn a spread on the currency the world holds

### Historical Reserve Currency Transitions

| Reserve Currency | Approximate Period | What Ended It |
|-----------------|-------------------|---------------|
| Portuguese real | 1450-1530 | Spanish ascendancy |
| Spanish real | 1530-1640 | Dutch commercial dominance |
| Dutch guilder | 1640-1720 | British military/commercial rise |
| British pound | 1720-1944 | WWI/WWII debt, US ascendancy |
| US dollar | 1944-present | ??? |

**The transition pattern:**
- Reserve currency status lags empire status by decades
- The pound remained dominant for years after Britain was no longer the world's largest economy
- Transitions are gradual (decades), not sudden (years)
- The new reserve currency must offer: liquidity, rule of law, deep capital markets, military backing

### Is the Dollar's Reserve Status at Risk?

**Arguments for stability:**
- No viable alternative yet (euro has structural issues, yuan lacks convertibility)
- US capital markets are deepest and most liquid
- US military is still dominant
- Network effects: everyone uses dollars because everyone uses dollars

**Arguments for erosion:**
- Rising US debt/GDP → potential currency debasement
- De-dollarization efforts by China, Russia, BRICS
- Weaponization of dollar (sanctions) gives countries incentive to diversify
- Digital currencies (including central bank digital currencies) could reduce dollar dependence

> "I'm not saying the dollar will lose its reserve status quickly. I'm saying the conditions that lead to loss of reserve status are forming, as they have in every prior case." — Dalio

## Investment Implications

### For Portfolio Construction

1. **Geographic diversification is essential**: Don't concentrate wealth in a single country, even the US. Empires decline, and domestic investors bear the brunt.

2. **Currency diversification**: Hold assets in multiple currencies. If your domestic currency is the reserve currency being challenged, diversification is even more important.

3. **Gold as empire-transition insurance**: Gold has maintained value across every reserve currency transition in the past 500 years. It's not an investment — it's insurance against the unknown.

4. **Real assets over financial assets**: During empire transitions, financial assets (bonds, bank deposits) denominated in the declining currency lose value. Real assets (land, commodities, businesses with pricing power) tend to preserve value.

### For Macro Analysis

When analyzing any country or region:
1. Where does it stand on the eight dimensions of power?
2. Is it rising or declining?
3. What is the internal conflict level? (Political polarization, wealth inequality)
4. What is the external conflict level? (Trade wars, military tensions)
5. What is the debt situation? (Sustainable or approaching crisis?)
6. What is the currency trajectory? (Strengthening or weakening?)
