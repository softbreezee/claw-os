# Currency Analysis and Sovereign Debt

> When to read this file: when analyzing currencies, evaluating sovereign debt sustainability, assessing central bank policy, or making cross-border investment decisions where currency risk is material.

> "Currencies are like anything else — they can be overvalued, undervalued, or fairly valued. And the forces that drive them are knowable, even if the timing isn't." — Dalio

---

## Currency Valuation Framework

### The Three Drivers

Dalio analyzes currencies through three lenses:

**1. Relative Productivity/Competitiveness (Long-Term Driver)**
Over decades, currencies appreciate for countries with rising productivity and depreciate for countries with declining productivity.

- **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)**: Does the currency allow you to buy the same goods/services at roughly the same cost as in other countries?
- **Unit labor cost trends**: Is the country becoming more or less competitive?
- **Innovation trajectory**: Is the country moving up or down the value chain?

**2. Capital Flows (Medium-Term Driver)**
Over years, currencies respond to flows of capital:

- **Trade flows**: Countries running current account surpluses accumulate foreign exchange reserves → their currency strengthens
- **Investment flows**: Countries attracting foreign investment see currency appreciation
- **Interest rate differentials**: Higher real interest rates attract capital → currency strengthens
- **Risk appetite**: "Risk-on" environments strengthen EM currencies; "risk-off" strengthens USD, JPY, CHF

**3. Central Bank Policy (Short-to-Medium-Term Driver)**
Central bank actions directly influence currency values:

- **Interest rate changes**: Rate hikes strengthen the currency; cuts weaken it
- **Money printing (QE)**: Increases money supply → weakens currency (all else equal)
- **Intervention**: Direct buying/selling of currency in forex markets
- **Forward guidance**: Signaling future policy direction

### Currency Health Indicators

| Indicator | Healthy | Warning | Crisis |
|-----------|---------|---------|--------|
| Current account | Surplus or small deficit (<3% GDP) | Deficit 3-5% GDP | Deficit >5% GDP |
| Foreign reserves | >6 months of imports | 3-6 months | <3 months |
| Real interest rate | Positive | Zero | Deeply negative |
| Inflation | 1-3% | 3-5% | >5% or deflation |
| Debt/GDP | <60% sovereign | 60-100% | >100% |
| External debt | <40% of GDP | 40-60% | >60% |

## Sovereign Debt Sustainability

### The Fundamental Equation

Sovereign debt is sustainable when:
```
Primary Balance + (Nominal GDP Growth - Nominal Interest Rate) × Debt/GDP ≥ 0
```

Simplified: Debt is sustainable if the economy grows faster than the interest rate on the debt, OR if the government runs a large enough primary surplus (revenue minus non-interest spending).

**When this equation breaks:**
- Interest rates rise above growth rates → debt spirals upward
- The government can't generate primary surpluses → must borrow to pay interest
- Creditors lose confidence → demand higher rates → accelerates the spiral

### The Five-Question Sovereign Assessment

1. **Can the country print its own money?**
   - Yes (US, UK, Japan) → can always avoid nominal default, but risk inflation
   - No (Eurozone members, dollarized economies) → can actually default

2. **Is the debt denominated in domestic or foreign currency?**
   - Domestic → can inflate away (less acute crisis risk)
   - Foreign → vulnerable to currency depreciation spirals (Argentina, Turkey)

3. **Who holds the debt?**
   - Domestic holders (Japan model) → more stable, less capital flight risk
   - Foreign holders → vulnerable to sudden stops in capital flows

4. **What is the fiscal trajectory?**
   - Deficits narrowing → debt/GDP will stabilize
   - Deficits widening → debt/GDP will accelerate
   - Key: Is there political will and ability to adjust?

5. **What is the growth potential?**
   - High growth (young demographics, rising productivity) → can grow out of debt
   - Low growth (aging demographics, stagnant productivity) → must inflate or restructure

## Central Bank Credibility Analysis

### What Makes a Central Bank Credible

Credibility is the central bank's most valuable asset — and once lost, extremely difficult to rebuild.

**The credibility framework:**

1. **Independence**: Is the central bank genuinely independent of political pressure?
   - Formal independence (laws) is necessary but not sufficient
   - Operational independence (ability to raise rates when government doesn't want it) is the real test

2. **Track record**: Has the central bank maintained price stability over time?
   - A central bank that has maintained 2% inflation for 20 years has enormous credibility
   - A central bank that allowed 30% inflation has zero credibility regardless of what it says now

3. **Communication**: Does the central bank communicate clearly and follow through?
   - "Forward guidance" only works if the central bank does what it says
   - Surprising the market destroys credibility

4. **Reserves**: Does the central bank have the resources to defend the currency if needed?
   - Large foreign reserves → credible defense against speculation
   - Minimal reserves → vulnerable to attack

### When Central Bank Credibility Fails

**The sequence:**
1. Government pressures central bank to finance spending
2. Central bank prints money to buy government bonds
3. Inflation rises, currency weakens
4. Central bank loses independence (formally or informally)
5. Investors lose confidence → demand higher rates on government debt
6. Higher rates → more fiscal pressure → more printing → currency collapse

**Historical examples:** Argentina (repeated), Turkey (2018-present), Zimbabwe, Weimar Germany

## Practical Currency Risk Management

### For International Investors

1. **Assess the currency risk**: Is the currency overvalued, undervalued, or fair?
2. **Quantify the exposure**: What percentage of portfolio returns comes from currency movements?
3. **Decide on hedging**: Full hedge, partial hedge, or unhedged?

**Hedging decision framework:**
| Situation | Hedge Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Investing in a stable, fairly-valued currency | Unhedged — currency adds diversification |
| Investing in an overvalued, weakening currency | Hedge — protect against depreciation |
| Investing in an undervalued, strengthening currency | Unhedged — currency gain adds to return |
| Investing in a country with potential debt/currency crisis | Hedge or avoid entirely |

### For Macro Positioning

Dalio uses currencies as both investment vehicles and risk indicators:

- **Buying undervalued currencies** of countries with strong fundamentals and rising competitiveness
- **Selling overvalued currencies** of countries with deteriorating fundamentals
- **Using currency movements as leading indicators**: Currency weakness often precedes broader economic deterioration

> "In my experience, currency movements are one of the best leading indicators of broader economic and financial market trends." — Dalio
